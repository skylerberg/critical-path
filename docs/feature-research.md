The 71 build rows resolve to **57 tasks** on the Critical Path board — several
research entries describe the same feature from different sections and share a

switching production email to SES, a general background job runner, and filtered

The pattern across the declines: anything that creates an ongoing obligation was
refused — no priority field, no estimates, no start dates, no digests, no inbox,
no saved views, no per-project toggles, no second view of the board. What was
accepted is either invisible until used, or infrastructure that makes an existing
feature pay off. Of the 86 features rated Skip or Anti-pattern, exactly one was
accepted, and only after being reshaped into something much smaller.

## How to read this

This document evaluates 251 features drawn from a survey of the tools in Critical Path's neighbourhood — Trello (before and after Atlassian), Asana, ClickUp, Jira, Linear, Basecamp, Notion, Todoist, Airtable, monday, Height, and the open-source kanban clones (Wekan, Planka, Nextcloud Deck, Plane, Focalboard) — weighted toward what users actually say in forums, issue trackers and vote counts rather than what vendors put on pricing pages. Each feature carries three judgements: **status** against Critical Path today (have / partial / missing, read off the current schema and routes), **fit** (core / strong / maybe / skip / anti-pattern), and **effort** (small / medium / large, meaning roughly a sitting, a week, or a project — backend plus frontend plus CLI). Status is factual; effort is a rough estimate that assumes the existing conventions (client-supplied ids, transaction middleware, `publishAfterCommit`) are reused rather than reinvented. Fit is the opinionated column: it is a judgement call about one specific goal — Trello as it was before it got heavy — and a different goal would reshuffle it completely. Where a feature is rated "anti-pattern" rather than merely "skip", that means the research contains people actively complaining about it in the products that shipped it, not just an absence of demand.

## The short version

The tools people love and the tools people abandon do not differ in feature count; they differ in whether the features impose obligations. Every feature that users unambiguously love is one that costs them nothing to leave empty — a keyboard shortcut, a drag that feels instant, a celebration animation, a colour, a count under a column heading. Every feature that users beg for and then resent is one that creates maintenance: custom fields nobody fills in, dates that must be kept true or the timeline lies, story points that only pay off through a chart, a notification matrix that exists because the defaults are wrong. That is the whole pattern behind the exits: people leave Jira over permission schemes and workflow validators, Asana over subtasks that its own filtered views can't see straight, ClickUp over being overwhelming, and Trello over a retroactive free-tier cut plus a 2025 redesign that broke the single predictable column of card-back buttons — four different accents on the same complaint, which is that the tool started serving people who don't do the work. Speed is the other half: sub-100ms local response is the single most-cited reason people switch tools, which makes it positioning rather than optimisation.

Critical Path sits well on the fundamentals and should not be modest about it: three nouns with drag ordering, flat membership with no roles (validated everywhere, complained about nowhere), per-project labels as the pressure valve that lets you refuse custom fields and priority for years, a constrained rich-text description that stops short of the block-editor tarpit, `is_done` as a rename-proof answer to a question other tools build whole status taxonomies for, realtime that actually works, and dependencies with cycle detection that survive a project copy — a differentiator no Trello clone has. The single biggest hole is that the product does not ship its own thesis: the dependency graph only pays the user back through `cpath ready`, so in the web app — where nearly all users will live — the namesake feature is a badge-less relation you can enter and never see again. The most urgent single defect is smaller and dumber: adding a member 404s if that email has never signed up, so you cannot invite anyone to a board. And the biggest parity gap, the one people cite as blocking migration off Trello, is that there is nowhere on a card to say anything — no comments, no history, no mentions — combined with the fact that nothing is recoverable: any member can delete the whole project, there is no per-card archive, no undo, and no record that anything ever existed.

## Build these next

1. **Pending invitations, claimable at signup** — the member-add endpoint 404s on an email with no account, so sharing a board today means asking someone to sign up first and tell you when they're done; this is the one item that stops a working product from being usable by two people, and it has to survive the invitee signing up with a different address than the one you typed, which is impossible while membership is keyed on exact-email match. (medium)
2. **The ready view and the board filter bar, in the board** — assignee/label/done/ready predicates that dim non-matching cards in place, with "ready" as a first-class chip; the predicates already exist in the CLI and the board payload is already fully client-side, so this is mostly rendering, and it is worth more than its size because it is the only place the dependency graph ever pays the user back. (small)
3. **Card comments** — a flat markdown stream on the card, which every competitor and every minimal open-source clone ships, is where a decision stops evaporating in chat, and it is the substrate that makes mentions, reactions, activity history and the entire notification story worth building at all. (medium)
4. **Stale-write guard on task edits** — an `updated_at` precondition returning 409 plus a "changed while you had it open" toast; without it a teammate's rich-text description vanishes silently, and with no history there is no way to even discover it happened. (small)
5. **Per-card archive, undo toast, and restore** — a third state between done and deleted so cards can leave the board reversibly; today the only exits are an irreversible cascade delete any member can perform and letting the Done column become a graveyard. (medium)
6. **@mentions plus direct-address email, routed strictly off involvement** — notify exactly one named person, email on exactly two events (added to a project, assigned a task), and never notify anyone about their own action; the email service and post-commit hooks already exist. (medium)
7. **Blocked / blocking affordances on the card face** — a badge that auto-clears and dim-in-place styling; the cheapest way to make the namesake feature felt by someone who will never open a terminal. (small)
8. **Changed since you last looked** — one `last_seen_at` per user per project driving a dot on the project list and a highlight on changed cards; awareness with no inbox, no email and no subscription model. (small)
9. **Global cross-project search** — one box over titles and descriptions across accessible projects with results that deep-link into the board; the gap that bites the first time a board outgrows one screen. (medium)
10. **Due dates, entered by typing** — an optional date with graded overdue colour, plus `fri` / `+3d` parsing rather than a calendar picker; the most-demanded missing feature in every board tool surveyed, and the typed entry is what keeps it from feeling like a form. (medium)
11. **"Your last blocker cleared"** — tell a task's assignee when its final blocker completes; the one genuinely high-signal alert available, and one no competitor can offer. (medium)
12. **Per-task activity history** — an append-only who/what/when/old→new log, which is the accountability substitute for the roles this product deliberately refuses to add. (medium)
13. **Duplicate a task, and bulk create from a multi-line paste** — a batch create endpoint is naturally idempotent because POST already takes a client-supplied id, and together these are the migration path off a spreadsheet. (small)
14. **Board legibility pack: per-column counts, card aging, image thumbnails, soft WIP limits** — four small independent wins on the surface people stare at all day, none of which asks the user to enter anything. (small)
15. **Filter state in the URL, plus one-key quick filters** — a query-string parser's worth of work that makes a filtered board shareable and bookmarkable, and replaces the entire saved-views feature every competitor paywalls. (small)
16. **Account integrity: email verification, leave a project, transfer ownership** — identity here is the email, `created_by` is NOT NULL with cascade, and the creator can neither leave nor be removed; one typo at signup currently means permanent lockout. (medium)
17. **Dark mode following the system preference** — three-way toggle applied before first paint; zero dissent anywhere in the research, and its absence reads as unfinished to a dev-leaning audience. (small)
18. **Responsive mobile web board with touch drag** — single-column swipe pager, long-press to pick up, full-screen card sheet; nobody praises responsive web and everybody punishes its absence. (medium)
19. **Human-readable task key (CP-142)** — a per-project counter in URLs, the CLI and copy-link; cheap schema, and the prerequisite for any git-hook recipe later. (medium)
20. **Free JSON export, then a Trello importer** — export is what buyers check before committing and Trello's paywalled CSV reads as hostage-taking; the importer is a wedge rather than parity, because Trello cannot re-import its own export. (large)

## The next tier

- **Board mechanics:** move a card or column to another project, reconciling project-scoped labels, assignees and dependencies — users treat a lossy move as a bug.
- **Board mechanics:** copy a card and duplicate a column, with per-element choices about what carries over — the comprehensible alternative to templates.
- **Board mechanics:** one-shot column sort by created/updated/title, and a setting for whether new cards land top or bottom.
- **Board mechanics:** move-all / archive-all the cards in a column; the column-delete endpoint already implements the hard half via `move_tasks_to`.
- **Board mechanics:** multi-select with bulk actions, shipped as batch endpoints so web, CLI and scripts share one primitive.
- **Dependencies:** a date-free dependency graph view — the clearest unmet demand in the category and it needs no dates at all.
- **Dependencies:** graph-derived ordering, "unblocks N others" and the longest unfinished chain, which earns the product its name without estimates.
- **Dependencies:** two-keystroke relation creation and drag-card-onto-card, plus cycle errors that print the offending path from the existing recursive CTE.
- **Dependencies:** person-level "who am I waiting on / whose work am I blocking" — one join away from existing data, and nobody else ships it.
- **Keyboard and clients:** single-key card shortcuts with a `?` cheat sheet, right-click parity, and a keyboard-operable Move to… that also serves mobile and accessibility.
- **Keyboard and clients:** a Cmd-K palette rendering the CLI's fuzzy entity resolver in the web, teaching shortcuts by printing them inline.
- **CLI:** shell completion, documented exit codes, `--jq` over `--json`, named profiles, and `cpath watch` tailing the existing WebSocket as NDJSON.
- **Access and platform:** an unlisted, revocable, noindex public read-only board link, plus an owner bit and a read-only member level.
- **Access and platform:** long-lived personal access tokens with published docs, and per-project HMAC-signed outbound webhooks over the events `publishAfterCommit` already emits.
- **Accessibility and delight:** alt text on task images (the one gap that cannot be retrofitted later), non-colour label encoding and reduced motion, a completion celebration, and a published page saying what this tool refuses to add.

## Do not build these

- **Typed custom fields** — the top-five request in every board tool and the precise mechanism by which each of them stopped being simple; labels already absorb 80% of the demand.
- **Subtasks and parent–child tasks** — the largest complexity multiplier in the catalogue, and every implementation leaks: filtered views, counts and dependency semantics all get the interaction wrong.
- **Swimlanes or any second grouping axis** — real demand everywhere, and the exact line at which a Trello-like starts to feel like Jira.
- **Configurable workflows with transitions, conditions and validators** — the machinery that makes Jira boards fight their users, to the point that Jira admins themselves conclude the least restrictive workflow works best.
- **Timeline / Gantt with critical path, float and auto-scheduling** — requires a duration on every task, gets called "plainly wrong and misleading" by the vendors' own users, and would be the most embarrassing possible failure for a product with this name.
- **A trigger→condition→action rule builder** — the highest bloat vector here: a rule editor, an execution log, ordering semantics, and then the usage metering every vendor added and users call bait-and-switch. Three hardcoded toggles beat it at 1% of the surface.
- **A per-event × per-channel notification preference matrix** — Jira's most hated subsystem; an elaborate preferences panel is an admission that your defaults generate too much.
- **Permission schemes and field- or column-level security** — "you can't click a damn thing without a permissions issue" is a direct quote, and every rule added becomes a support request only you can resolve.
- **Widget dashboards, portfolios and OKR rollups** — a second product bolted onto the first, serving people who don't do the work; no individual contributor anywhere is on record loving them, and when OKRs live next to tasks, tasks always win.
- **The card as a full document** — Notion-style nested blocks and sub-pages inside a card is a different product with a well-documented editor-UX tarpit, and the constrained Tiptap description is already at the right point.
- **A second task surface beside the board** — Basecamp's to-dos-vs-card-table split is the clearest anti-recommendation in the research: a second place to put a task is worse than ten new fields on the first.
- **Per-item assignees and due dates on checklist items** — the moment a checklist silently becomes a second task system that no view, filter or dependency can see.
- **Built-in chat, and an in-product AI assistant** — teams keep Slack because it is always open, so in-app chat competes with your own comments and loses; and in-product AI is what users name in both the Trello and Linear bloat backlashes, while Height died betting on it.
- **Per-workspace feature toggles or a second "simplified" project type** — configurability is what bloat looks like when you're too polite to say no; ClickUp shipped the toggles and still reads as a mess. Defaults are the product.

## A possible order of work

**Phase 1 — Fix the front door.** The things whose absence loses a user in the first week, most of them small.
Pending invitations claimable at signup (including a different email); email verification; leave a project and transfer ownership; stale-write guard on task edits; per-card archive with undo toast and restore.

**Phase 2 — Make the name true.** Cash the dependency graph inside the product, not just the CLI.
Ready view and board filter bar in the web; blocked/blocking badges with dim-in-place; cycle errors that print the path; "your last blocker cleared"; confirm-on-done for blocked work; person-level waiting-on view.

**Phase 3 — Give the work a voice.** The parity gap that blocks migrations, plus the cheapest possible awareness layer.
Card comments; @mentions notifying one person; direct-address email on assign and add-to-project; involvement-only routing with a per-project mute; per-task activity history; changed-since-you-last-looked; emoji reactions.

**Phase 4 — Find it and move it.** The board stops being the only way to see anything.
Global cross-project search; filter state in the URL; one-key quick filters; cross-project My Tasks; human-readable task keys; duplicate a task; bulk create from a multi-line paste; the legibility pack (column counts, card aging, image thumbnails, soft WIP limits).

**Phase 5 — Dates, without becoming a calendar.** Add the one obligation worth adding, and stop there.
Due date with graded overdue colour; typed natural-language entry; due-soon and overdue reminders reusing Phase 3's routing; a read-only iCal feed; auto-archive of stale completed work.

**Phase 6 — Everywhere else.** Reach, trust and taste.
Responsive mobile board with touch drag; dark mode; single-key shortcuts and the command palette; installable PWA; JSON export and the Trello importer; public read-only board link; personal access tokens and outbound webhooks; alt text and the accessibility baseline; the published method page and pricing promise.

# The full catalogue

## The card and its content

The card is where a simple tool either stays simple or dies. Every product surveyed here — Trello, Jira, Asana, monday, ClickUp, Notion, and a dozen open-source clones — is recognisably the same board; what separates them is how much stuff they let you hang off a single card. Critical Path's card today is unusually lean (title, rich-text description, assignees, labels, images, dependencies) and lean in mostly the right places, but it is missing the one thing nobody in any corpus argues about — dates — while being pressured toward the two things that reliably end simplicity: custom fields and subtask hierarchies. ### Worth reading closely

**Dates are the one gap nobody will forgive.** This is the least negotiable finding in the whole corpus. Nextcloud Deck's "mark card as done" thread (78 👍, 96 comments) escalates from "Any progress so far?" to "Seriously, how to mark a card as DONE??? Why is this request ignored since July 2018?"; a Planka user wrote "I just updated our Planka to v2.0.0 and instantly went to check if notifications for due dates are included... sadly that doesn't seem to be the case," and got "Still sad to see this essential feature not being implemented" in reply. The implementation details that separate good from bad are cheap: an optional single date, a badge that grades through three or four colours (Linear's red-today / orange-this-week / grey is enough — Trello's five-state ladder is the maximalist version), and the ability to tick it complete from the board. The trap is the second-order surface: Asana forum posts complain about "suddenly receiving Inbox notifications for all upcoming tasks when a specific due time is set," and Deck users report being unable to stop reminders for finished work except by deleting the date. Ship dates without any notification machinery first, and make an undated card look exactly like it does today — the counter-sentiment is real, with one HN commenter dismissing task apps that are "too opinionated about how you deal with your todo items (like forcing schedules, reminders or due dates)."

**Date *input* matters more here than in any GUI tool, because of the CLI.** A nine-year Things user named as his top reason to shop around: "Speed of date picking and moving todos... It's 2024 guys, we don't need calendar pickers when we have command palettes." Things accepts `tod`, `2p`, `10-3`, `17d`, `3rd wed jul`; Todoist parses dates inline in quick add. For `cpath`, `--due fri` and `--due +3d` should work on day one, and the same parser should back the web input. Nudge keys (±1 day, ±1 week) are the other half — rescheduling is a far more frequent operation than scheduling.

**Checklists, not subtasks — and this is the single most consequential structural choice in the section.** The evidence for the cheap version is unusually direct. HN's smackay: "Checklists are infinitely better for subtasks... all information and all progress is forced to be on one card." r00fus: "you can make a task a checklist of sub-tasks very easily without promoting it or dealing with a tree interface." The evidence against the heavy version is louder still: Jira's IshKebab — "You should basically never use Epics or Subtasks in Jira because they have weird unnecessary restrictions that don't apply to normal tasks"; fragmede — subtasks are "a constantly leaky abstraction, some actions can only be done against tasks and others only subtasks." Asana's "allow subtasks to be added to the parent's project" thread has 403 replies and 47,282 views purely because subtasks don't show up in the views people actually look at. Hierarchy is also the largest complexity multiplier in Critical Path's own architecture: positions, done-ness, dependency resolution, access checks and realtime events all become recursive. Shortcut's ordering is the model to copy — checklists first, real sub-tasks only later and opt-in, with a one-click "convert checklist to sub-tasks" — and the escape hatch alone (promote an item to a card in the same column) buys most of the value at a fraction of the cost. Do not follow Trello into per-item assignees and due dates: that is the point where a checklist becomes a second task system that no view knows about, and it forced Trello to build a whole cross-board "Your Items" screen just to make the data findable again.

**A short task key is the highest-leverage cheap addition for a CLI-first product.** Jira's whole git integration "is string matching. Include PROJ-123 in a branch name, commit message, or PR title, and Jira picks it up." Linear binds `Cmd+Shift+.` to copying a branch name containing the ID. Asana's lack of one is a standing forum request ("F9: User-friendly task addresses" — "Current task URLs are long and unwieldy," with workarounds as sad as bit.ly), and an HN commenter rejecting Trello listed the gap first: "There is no task number, there are no subtasks." Critical Path's CLI currently compensates with fuzzy entity references — a key makes the common case exact instead of fuzzy, and makes `#142` autocomplete in descriptions possible. The one dissenting datapoint is worth honouring: Vikunja has an open request titled "Make task id less prominent in kanban." So mint a per-project counter, put it in the URL, the CLI, the copy-link output and the card back — and keep it off, or very quiet on, the card face.

**Custom fields are the mechanism by which every tool in this survey became the thing you don't want to be.** They are simultaneously the most-demanded item (62 👍 on Plane, 35 on Nextcloud Deck, 16 on Vikunja, 13 on Planka) and the best-documented self-inflicted wound: at 1,400 Jira custom fields JQL search takes ~3 seconds, at 2,800 it takes ~9; admins "guess they have somewhere between 50 and 100 custom fields, then open the admin screen and find 400." HN, on Jira: "it's so dead simple for braindead process zombies to cram another field, another label, another required text box." On the Notion side: "Most rollup data goes completely unused. People spend hours configuring formulas that calculate metrics they check once at setup and never again." The important structural insight for Critical Path is that labels are the pressure valve — "a product with labels can credibly say no to custom fields for years" — and Critical Path already has per-project labels plus a rich description, which absorb the great majority of real small-team custom-field usage. If the pressure ever becomes unbearable, GitLab's scoped labels (`priority::high` auto-removing `priority::low`) buy single-select enums for one uniqueness constraint and no new UI. That is the correct next step, not a field editor.

**Attachments being image-only is a genuine outlier.** No other tool in the cohort ships that restriction, and the everyday non-technical use is a PDF quote, a signed contract, or a spreadsheet on the card. The two ergonomics users name explicitly are clipboard paste ("Paste image on clipboard directly to comments like GitHub," 32 👍 on Deck) and drag-onto-the-card; Jira users treat screenshot-paste as the single most-used path for filing a bug. Two adjacent notes. First, Trello's plain-URL attachment — a link stored as an attachment that renders a title and favicon — is underrated and nearly free. Second, security: Trello moved uploaded attachments off unguessable URLs to board-authorised URLs in December 2023, after its 2020 public-board exposure and the January 2024 leak of 15M+ email addresses. Critical Path serves images from unguessable capability URLs today; the moment cards start carrying contracts and invoices, that decision is worth revisiting.

**Priority is the field to keep refusing, and Critical Path has a better answer than most.** Jira's priority field produces the well-known drift where everything becomes High, and it is one of the fields most cited in mandatory-field-bloat complaints; Things 3 is among the most-loved task apps in existence and ships no priority field at all, telling users to use tags instead. The strongest argument is structural and specific to this product: as one commenter put it in the dependency research, determining what is *available* should precede determining what is *important*. Critical Path's dependency graph and CLI "ready" list already answer "what should I do next" with information the user didn't have to invent, and column position already encodes ordering. A priority enum would add a third, competing source of truth. If it is ever built, copy Linear exactly — five fixed levels, no renaming, no adding — because Linear's docs are right that "adding too many options makes it harder to set priority," and no user in any source asked for a sixth level.

**Finally, a warning about the card back itself, not any field on it.** Trello's May 2025 redesign broke the card's single vertical column of action buttons into multiple regions, and it became the most-cited grievance of the entire redesign: "four different places to look for what used to be one column of buttons"; "Now there are lot of unnecessary clicks everywhere thanks to the UI redesign"; adding a member "now requires navigating through multiple clicks" where it used to be one. Predictability of button position beat visual freshness by a wide margin. Every feature recommended above — dates, checklists, file attachments, links — competes for space on that surface. Decide now that they go into one column in a fixed order, and that the card back never becomes a dashboard.

### Skip these

- **Per-checklist-item assignees and due dates** — turns checklists into a shadow task system that no board view can see, and forces you to build a cross-board "my items" screen to compensate.
- **Subtasks / parent–child tasks** — the single largest complexity multiplier available (positions, done-ness, dependencies, realtime events all become recursive), and every implementation surveyed is described by its own users as leaky or second-class.
- **Epics or any grouping layer above the card** — projects and columns already do this at this scale; Shortcut users report stories not populating into roadmap views, and evaluators report not knowing when to use an epic versus a story.
- **Typed custom fields** — the highest-demand, highest-danger item in the catalogue; it is precisely how Trello became monday.com, and per-project labels plus a rich description already cover small-team needs.
- **Formula, rollup and auto-progress fields** — computed metrics that "people check once at setup and never again," with silent correctness traps (Notion truncates formulas to the top 25 related pages).
- **Mirrored / live-synced values from linked cards** — nobody in the research praised any implementation; mirrors reliably break filters, search and automations, and they need a full relation model underneath.
- **Workspace-wide tags parallel to per-project labels** — two overlapping classification systems is Asana's documented onboarding confusion; keep exactly one.
- **Priority enum** — a third source of truth alongside column position and the dependency graph, and it drifts to High everywhere it exists.
- **Estimates / story points** — buys velocity charts nobody in this product asked for and invites the abuse HN describes ("charts and calling people on the carpet for missing their estimates").
- **Time tracking, timesheets and billable flags** — a market-segment decision, not a feature decision; salaried ICs call it "justification for aggressive micromanagement," and if agency users ever demand it, Basecamp's paid-add-on model or a Toggl/Harvest integration beats a core field.
- **Card as a full document (block editor)** — 50+ block types is a different product, and the constrained Tiptap description already sits at the right point on that curve.
- **Milestones as a distinct task type** — a PM checkbox feature with no timeline view to render it on and no organic enthusiasm in any source.
- **Card types with per-type templates** — the `is_template` column was already dropped deliberately; teams argue endlessly about whether something is a Feature or a Chore, and template schemas are the front edge of "initial complexity overwhelming."
- **Draft-only cards** — GitHub's drafts don't send notifications and can't carry labels, which its own community calls confusing; one task type is a real asset, don't spend it.

## Board structure and card manipulation

This is the part of the product that *is* the product. Everything else in Critical Path — dependencies, labels, realtime, the CLI — hangs off a board of ordered columns holding ordered cards, and the pre-Atlassian Trello lesson is that the spine should stay boring while the affordances around it get sharp. The research shows the highest-value work here is not new structure (swimlanes, workspaces, status taxonomies) but *safety and mechanics*: Critical Path today has no per-card archive, no undo, no trash and no activity log, which means the only way to clear a finished card is an irreversible `DELETE` on a schema that cascades. ### Worth reading closely

**Archive, undo and delete are one cluster, and it is the riskiest gap in the product.** Right now a card can only leave the board by being deleted, and delete on a fully-cascading schema is final — no trash, no undo, no activity log to reconstruct from. Asana users maintain an entire feature-request thread ("F16: Archive tasks", with three ugly workarounds) precisely because marking abandoned work *complete* corrupts your done column while deleting destroys context; Planka shipped card archive after a 13-👍 request; Deck has a separate 13-👍 request just for a listing of archived cards. Trello's design is the one to copy: archive is the default action, delete is only reachable *from* the archive. Two implementation details matter. First, keep archived rows in `task` with an `archived_at` and exclude them at the view layer — GitHub's docs admit "Insights does not track items you have archived or deleted," which is exactly the trap to avoid. Second, an archive with no way to browse it is a black hole, and Critical Path has no search; so the archive needs at minimum a per-project "archived cards" listing endpoint and a CLI subcommand, or people will fear it. On the other side, note the Trello mobile complaint — users "uniformly frustrated by the absence of a feature to directly delete boards and cards, forced to archive unwanted items instead" — so archive must not become the *only* path.

**Undo is worth more than confirmation dialogs, and Critical Path's transaction discipline makes it cheap.** The HN consensus is unusually strong: barrkel calls confirm dialogs "pointless and annoying", antimagic calls them "a UI smell", jrockway nominates Gmail's inline undo as strictly superior. The dissent is worth respecting — mechanical_fish's synthesis is that genuinely catastrophic actions deserve *both* a typed confirmation and a recovery path. That maps cleanly onto this product: card archive gets an undo toast (undo = flip `archived_at` back, one PATCH); project delete — which today any member can perform on a project they didn't create — gets a type-the-name confirmation plus a soft-delete window. Also worth noting: `DELETE /columns/:id` already refuses to destroy cards silently and demands `move_tasks_to`. That instinct is right; extend it rather than dilute it.

**Soft WIP limits are the single best effort-to-enthusiasm ratio in this section.** It is one integer on `board_column` plus a count you are already fetching for the board view, and the sentiment is the most emphatic in the entire research set. regularfry: "WIP limits are fundamental to kanban. They're why kanban works." wpietri runs "a WIP limit of 3 for active tasks, and three for pending". sleepychu's team uses "engineers/2+1". theshackleford credits it with "significant success" managing ADHD — "it clicked with my brain". brc's specific complaint about Trello is that there is "no way to set a WIP limit on any one list", and the same request appears from akurilin in 2013 and from prxtl about Linear in 2026 — two independent users, a decade apart, asking for the same constraint. Every account agrees the limit must be *soft*: colour the header, never block the drop, because hard blocks make people park work off-board. Ship it together with the per-column card count, since the count is the denominator anyway and is independently the cheapest "reporting" feature that exists.

**Card aging is nearly free here and is the most on-brand feature in the catalogue.** `task.updated_at` already exists. Two lines of derived opacity gives you what the Kanban Guide calls the most actionable flow metric: "Work Item Age tells you what is going wrong right now, not what went wrong last month." Trello's version is the most-cited example of its pre-acquisition personality (Pirate Mode makes stale cards crack and tear like a treasure map), and I found no complaints about it anywhere. One design decision to make consciously: `updated_at` fires on any edit, including a label change, so a card can be "fresh" while sitting in In Progress for three weeks. The truer signal is a `column_entered_at` timestamp set whenever `column_id` changes — a single nullable column, one line in the task update handler, and it also gives you a start boundary for cycle time later without committing to any chart. Make aging opt-in per project so nobody gets a guilt-trip they didn't ask for.

**Bulk actions should reuse the primitives you already have, not grow their own UI.** Linear's lesson is compositional: `X` to select, `Cmd+A` to take everything visible, `Cmd+K` to act — bulk editing needs no dedicated screen because selection and the command palette compose. Jira's counter-example is the wizard, which admins describe as "just a PITA". Two traps from the trackers: Kanboard #3916 documents that a bulk "close all in column" that ignores the active filter is terrifying, and monday deliberately records a batch as *one* undoable entry — bulk edit without undo is the single most likely source of an irreversible mistake. For Critical Path specifically the API shape matters more than the UI: a small number of batch endpoints (set column, add/remove label, set assignees, archive) that run in the existing transaction middleware and emit one realtime event per affected task will serve the CLI, the web app and any scripting the owner does, whereas a client-side loop of N PATCHes will produce N websocket events and a visibly stuttering board for everyone else in the project.

**Card sort and new-card placement are tiny, unglamorous, and users complain about them for years.** Nextcloud Deck carries *two separate* 24-👍 issues about nothing but where a new card lands, plus a 26-👍 request to sort a list by date; Planka shipped "create cards at top" as a named v2 highlight. Build the one-shot version: a sort action that rewrites positions once and then leaves manual dragging as the default. A persistent sort *mode* fights with drag-and-drop and creates the question "why did my card jump back". With float positions this is a single UPDATE per card, and the sort keys available today are created_at, updated_at and title — which is enough.

**Cross-project card movement is the gap that will bite once someone runs two boards.** Users treat a lossy move as a bug, not a limitation: Kanboard #4407 ("duplicating task to another project removes comments and attachments") is filed as a defect, Plane #2733 ("unable to transfer a task to another project") has 11 👍, and Deck's "move card to another board" request carried a $20 bounty and 24 👍. Critical Path's model makes the semantics genuinely interesting rather than trivial: labels are project-scoped (reconcile by name+colour, create what's missing — and *say* what you did), assignees may not be members of the target project (drop them and report it), and dependencies are the namesake feature and would either have to be severed or the model extended to cross-project blocking. Basecamp's constraint is the elegant dodge worth stealing: instead of a move-anywhere picker, let a project declare up to four "wormhole" destinations. That is a small feature that covers the real workflow (design → engineering handoff) without a project browser inside a card modal.

**Structure above the project is the thing to keep resisting — and this repo has already made that call once.** Migration 0002 added workspaces and 0010 removed them, which was the right instinct: ClickUp's Space→Folder→List hierarchy is credited with letting teams "retire Notion, Trello, and Excel" and is *simultaneously* the origin of an entire consulting genre about not getting it wrong. Trello's own answer at scale is weak (Collections are paywalled and shallow) and the 2025 sidebar removal produced genuine fury. But board proliferation is a real failure mode — Deck's "board grouping" request has 30 👍 and "set default board on start" 24 👍 — and the cheap answer is the one Trello got right in 2011: starring. Critical Path already stores a per-user project ordering, so a per-user `starred` boolean on the same table is a few hours of work and buys most of what a folder hierarchy would, with none of the "which container does this live in" tax.

### Skip these

- **Swimlanes (persistent lane rows):** genuine demand across trackers, but this is precisely the line where a Trello-like starts feeling like Jira, and Kanboard's follow-on requests ("different columns across swimlanes", "column grouping/split columns") show how fast it metastasises. If the itch gets loud, ship collapsible group-by-label *within* a column as a personal view setting instead — same 80%, no second axis in the schema.
- **Board columns derived from a field instead of stored column rows:** GitLab's label-driven lists produce exactly the confusion you'd expect ("compared to JIRA Agile the issue board is a joke… it either doesn't work correctly"), and stored columns are what makes drag-and-drop mean something obvious.
- **Prescribed status taxonomies (fixed states, status categories, Deliver→Accept/Reject):** you already have the good half — a one-bit `is_done` on columns. Adding a category system means teaching users a vocabulary before they can name a column, which is the opposite of "five sticky notes on a whiteboard".
- **Configurable workflows with transitions, conditions and validators:** the Jira admins who lived it concluded "making the workflows the least restrictive possible was the most effective" because otherwise "everyone hates using it" and the data gets *worse*. And as one HN commenter put it, process non-compliance "is a people problem, and it isn't going to be solved in the Jira workflow editor".
- **Mirrored / multi-homed cards:** one card on many boards breaks every project-scoped concept you have — labels, members, dependencies, the realtime room model, and the 404-on-inaccessible authorization rule. Trello's mirror feature generated almost no organic enthusiasm despite being shipped and marketed; ClickUp's version has standing open complaints about status collisions.
- **Workspaces, folders and deep container hierarchy:** already tried and reverted in this codebase. Hierarchy converts a personal tool into an organisational one and destroys time-to-first-value; starring plus the existing per-user ordering covers the actual pain.
- **Rule-driven card colouring and colour stripes:** the rules would have to key off priority, due dates or custom fields — none of which exist, and none of which should be added just to feed a colouring engine. Per-project labels already give the board a colour language, manually and comprehensibly.
- **Classes of service / expedite lanes:** a taxonomy that requires a training course to explain. The mechanism that survives in real tools is "a red label plus an agreement that only one is in flight", which the existing labels already deliver.
- **A second task surface beside the board:** Basecamp's To-dos-vs-Card-Tables split is the clearest anti-recommendation in the whole research set — reviewers report "every single time they go to use Basecamp, they have to search for the features they want". Adding a second place to hold a task is more damaging than adding ten fields to the first one.

## Dependencies, blockers and critical path

This is the one category where Critical Path is not competing — it is already ahead of Trello, Basecamp, Notion and GitHub Projects, and it is the reason the product has its name. The research is unusually clear on two points: dependencies are the feature that separates "something you can use on complex projects" from "a mere to-do list" (darkteflon, HN), and every big tool treats them as a stored fact rather than something the product acts on. That gap is the whole opportunity. The trap is equally clear: everything downstream of *dates* — lag, auto-shifting, float, Gantt critical path — is where the incumbents earn their worst reviews, and Critical Path has no dates, which is a feature, not a deficiency.

### Worth reading closely

**The namesake is a trap in exactly one direction.** Asana's "critical path" is the most technically criticised feature in the entire research set: forum users say it "highlights the path through task C as the critical path, which is plainly wrong and misleading", that it picks arbitrarily between tied paths, and that Asana "should not describe this feature as critical path" — all because there is no duration field, so there is no float, so there is no CPM. Do not follow them. But there is an honest, dateless version nobody ships: in a graph with no durations, the critical path is simply the longest chain of unfinished dependent tasks, and it is exactly computable from what is already in `task_dependency`. Highlighting that chain, and giving every task an "unblocks N others" count derived from transitive reachability, gives the product a defensible claim to its own name with no dates, no estimates and no priority field. Asana users asked for the second half of this directly ("Create new aggregate field called Blocker Tasks" — tasks that block something but are themselves unblocked), and ClickUp's 850-vote graph request justifies itself as "easy identification of bottlenecks and critical paths".

**The visual half is the highest-leverage work left.** The data model is done; the affordances are not. GitLab's version is the reference and it is small: an icon next to the title in lists and on the board, which disappears automatically when the blocker closes, plus a confirmation when you close a still-blocked issue. It attracts no complaints anywhere in the research, which is the signal. The details that separate good from bad are specific — the badge must distinguish *blocked-by* from *blocking* (ClickUp's "Color of dependency symbol", 38 votes, complains the current colours are "counterintuitive"), and hovering must name the actual blocker and its status rather than saying "waiting on dependency" (a separate ClickUp request). On hide-versus-dim there is a genuine split: Taskwarrior users love that it "greys out whatever I can't do yet without actually hiding it", while Asana users beg to hide blocked tasks entirely ("we cannot work on them yet"). Support both; default to dim, because dimming never lies about what is on the board.

**Both directions are stored but only one is rendered.** `cpath task blockers --tree` walks upstream; there is no downstream equivalent, and `cpath task show` prints "Blocked by:" with no "Blocking:" section. That is precisely the Asana complaint — "in task view it would be helpful to see what the task is blocking, not just what it's being blocked by" — and it is a one-afternoon fix given the board payload already contains every edge. Rendering the downstream side is also what makes leverage legible: seeing "this blocks 4 things" on the card is what turns a dependency from trivia into a scheduling argument.

**The unblock notification is the most-complained-about missing behaviour in this whole space, and Asana's implementation is a case study in getting the suppression rule wrong.** Asana deliberately does not notify you when you complete a blocker on a task you are also assigned, on the theory that you already know. Users reject that flatly: Katriel Friedman — "One of the things I want out of dependencies is a foolproof way of reminding myself to get started on something when another condition is met"; RSDA points out that if you complete the blocker from another project you never see the dependent unblock at all, so it is simply lost. If Critical Path builds notifications, "your last blocker just cleared" should be the first one, it should fire regardless of who cleared it, and the realtime bus already gives you the delivery path — the missing substrate is a notification concept, not an event.

**Advisory, always — but advisory does not mean silent.** monday ships Flexible / Strict / No action modes, which is a confession that the vendor could not decide; Smartsheet's "strict" behaviour is so hostile that manually editing a dependent's date "overrides the association with the predecessor and removes the predecessor value" — the link vanishes without a prompt. The community consensus is Porrumentzio's on the Nextcloud Deck thread: whether to start blocked work "is a human decision". Jira agrees by construction — links never restrict transitions. The right middle ground is GitLab's: never prevent, but confirm on completing blocked work, and expose the contradictions that already exist (a task sitting in a done column whose blocker is not done). That is OmniPlan's "resolving violations" idea stripped of the scheduling engine, and it is cheap because the check is a single query.

**Cycle detection is already there; the error message is the product.** `src/services/dependencies.ts` runs a recursive CTE upstream from the proposed blocker and answers a boolean, which the handler turns into a generic 409. Nobody in the industry does better — ClickUp's "Warning for Circular Dependencies" is marked Not on roadmap, and MS Project's message is terse. Returning the actual path from that same CTE ("A already blocks C, which blocks B") costs one changed `select` and makes the failure self-explanatory in both the CLI and the web UI. This is the cheapest way to look more serious about graphs than anyone else in the category.

**A dependency graph view is the clearest unmet demand in the research, and it must not be a Gantt.** ClickUp's request has run since 2019 with 850 votes; commenters insist it be separate from Gantt "because you know the workflow but not the dates", cite Obsidian's graph plugin as the model, and one says the absence drove them off the platform. Asana's equivalent thread has run since April 2018 with no official response, and one user shipped a Python script emitting DOT diagrams as a workaround. Shortcut's version — Mermaid diagrams rendered on the story and across Epics, shipped Feb 2025 — is the pragmatic shape, and Wekan's "red strings" the maximal one. The honest caveat is legibility: "Gantt view is nice for this but becomes difficult to decipher when dependencies start to overlap", so scope the render to the connected component around a card rather than the whole board.

**The external-blocker question has a better answer here than a new entity.** Half of real blockers are a vendor, a sign-off or another team's tracker, and Azure DevOps is the only mainstream tool with named semantics for it. But Critical Path's cards are cheap, support assignees, descriptions and a done column, and already participate in the ready computation — "make a card called *client sign-off*" is a genuinely good answer that costs zero new concepts, zero new UI and no manual "cleared" toggle for people to forget. Only build a second blocker entity if the owner sees users repeatedly modelling non-work as work. The same reasoning applies to cross-project blockers: GitLab's access-to-both visibility rule maps cleanly onto the 404 model, but it complicates the board payload, the ready computation and the realtime access re-check all at once — real cost, thin demand, wait for the ask.

### Skip these

- **Blocked column / "On Hold" sub-sections** — a Blocked column destroys the fact that a card was In Progress; ClickUp users specifically complain about "cycling through 'Blocked' status unnecessarily". Users can already make one by convention; an orthogonal badge is strictly better and you are already positioned to ship it.
- **Full typed link taxonomy (relates / clones / causes / implements) and duplicate-merge** — a ten-item dropdown makes the common case slower to serve semantics nothing acts on; Atlassian's own docs concede links "do not affect workflow status or transitions". No user in the research asked a simple tool for more link types; they asked for blocking to *do* more.
- **Blocker reason taxonomy and blocked-time clustering charts** — a monthly retrospective analytics ritual for enterprise kanban teams, with no user testimony behind it. If blocked duration ever matters, `task_dependency.created_at` already answers it without a taxonomy.
- **Dependency-triggered automation rules** — a rules builder is precisely the bloat Trello acquired. The one valuable trigger ("last blocker cleared") is better shipped as a notification than as a general engine; HN on monday: "endless flexibility… leads them to just waste a ton of money".
- **Per-task dependency caps** — Asana's 30/45 limits generate nothing but complaints, and they surface at the exact moment a user is modelling something genuinely complex. Keep the current absence of a cap.
- **Making dependencies interact with a subtask hierarchy** — this is a request to add subtasks, and every tool that has both answers the interaction inconsistently, corrupting filtered views. Having no second tree is an advantage; containment is already expressible as a blocking edge.
- **FS / SS / FF / SF types, lag and lead, auto-shifting dates, strict enforcement modes** — three of the four types exist only to serve auto-scheduling, and lag is meaningless without dates. Asana's auto-shift generates its densest complaint cluster (wrong arithmetic, works in one view only, propagates downstream only) and Smartsheet's silently deletes the link you created.
- **Gantt critical path, float/slack and dependency arrows** — requires a duration on every task, a Gantt view, and a tolerance for being wrong; the tools that ship it are the ones users trust least on it. gregjor, forty years in: "I have never seen this work… no matter how talented the team."
- **Baselines, Monte Carlo simulation, critical-chain buffers and resource leveling** — PMO machinery that presumes estimates a board-first tool will never have, and that its own practitioners describe as invalidated within weeks of being produced.

## Views, planning and reporting

The honest split: a handful of these answer "what should I work on next, across every board I'm on," which is a real day-one question that Critical Path today answers only in the CLI; the rest are manager reporting surfaces, and they are precisely the features users name when they describe a tool getting worse. ### Worth reading closely

**The three Core items are one feature wearing three hats, and you already wrote the hard part.** `cli/src/commands/task.ts` has the predicates — assignee, label, column, done/not-done, ready, blocked — and `cli/src/board.ts` already computes `done | ready | blocked` per task. A board filter bar is those predicates on the board; a "Ready" view is one of them pinned; "My Tasks" is the same predicates run across every accessible project instead of one. The only genuinely new thing is a cross-project read (a bulk query over accessible projects, in the spirit of the existing `jsonArrayFrom` project list). Ship the filter bar first, because everything else is a saved instance of it.

**Copy Linear's My Issues ordering, not a sort dropdown.** Linear's default "Assigned" tab is not sorted by one field — it buckets in a deliberate sequence: urgent, SLA-bound, *things you are blocking*, current-cycle, backlog. The design lesson is that the right ordering mixes several signals, so exposing it as "sort by" makes it worse. Critical Path has an unusually good date-free version of that signal available: tasks that block other people's tasks, then ready, then blocked. That ordering is computable today from the dependency edges and needs no due dates, no priority field and no estimates. Leantime's much-praised "My Work" makes the same point subtractively — users said "I've finally found a tool that works the way my brain works," and the reason is what it hides. Two failure modes to avoid, both documented: ClickUp sorts overdue *below* today (power users call it backwards), and Asana forced Today/Upcoming/Later on people and got pages of "It's *my tasks*. Let me manage them the way that works best for me" and "This honestly is a deal breaker and we will be looking for another product." Asana capitulated and made the sections user-owned. Hardcode the bucket order if you must, but let people re-order within it.

**Saved views rot unless someone owns them, and the personal-vs-shared question is a real product decision.** Linear puts an explicit owner on every view, displayed in the sidebar — that single detail is what stops a workspace accumulating an unowned graveyard of stale filters. Airtable splits views three ways (collaborative / personal / locked) because in a shared base one collaborator re-sorting a grid changes it for everyone; Notion solves the same problem per-change with "Save for everyone" vs keep-personal. For a tool with flat, role-free membership, personal-by-default with an explicit "share with the project" is the cheapest safe answer. Jira's cautionary tale is permissions: shared filters can reference projects the viewer can't see, and "All the gadgets are empty" is a common response to a shared dashboard — Critical Path's 404-not-403 rule means a shared view must re-check access per row, exactly like the realtime delivery layer already does.

**Calendar, timeline, Today, Upcoming, burndown and workload are all the same purchase: dates.** Every one of them is worthless until a date field exists and is diligently filled in on most tasks, and the products that shipped them anyway generate a permanent support burden — Jira's docs and forums are dominated by "why is my issue not on the calendar," which is always the missing-date problem. The independent commentary on the low-effort versions is brutal: a Vikunja user found the Gantt chart "not at all usable"; a Businessmap reviewer said "the planning view is not yet usable for us." Notion's own timeline docs concede it needs a date *range* property, not a due date. So don't evaluate "calendar view" on its own merits — it's a downstream consequence of the dates decision made in another section, and buying it piecemeal gets you the support burden without the payoff.

**The Trello Inbox and Planner are the best-documented example of how this category ruins a product, and the sin was the default, not the idea.** Forum reaction: "The new trello has so much clutter, like that whole inbox thing that now takes up the whole left side"; "How to remove Inbox and Planner from page view. I can hardly see my boards!"; one user reported the Planner "takes up 2/3rds of my Trello board," called it "a ridiculous feature," and said they preferred "the simple old interface of a TODO list rather than something like Notion or Jira." People had to be told the `g+i` shortcut to hide it. Commentators framed it as strategic drift — Trello abandoning the niche between to-do apps and heavyweight PM tools to compete with Sunsama. If Critical Path ever ships a personal surface, the rule that falls out of this is absolute: it is a route you navigate to, never a panel that eats board real estate, and never on by default.

**If you ship exactly one piece of reporting, make it card aging, and compute it live.** Practitioners are blunt that aging is the metric that changes behaviour where cycle time doesn't: "Cycle Time makes you feel informed. Age makes you feel accountable," because aging is about live cards you can still act on. It's also nearly free here — `task.updated_at` already exists, so a subtle visual cue on cards untouched for N days needs no new tables. Everything richer needs history you don't store: a cumulative flow diagram requires a daily snapshot of counts per column, and that snapshot table is the actual reason simple tools don't have one. Note also GitHub's documented flaw — "Insights does not track items you have archived or deleted," so its charts quietly rewrite history when you tidy up. And the demand signal in your actual cohort is conspicuously absent: no upvoted analytics request in Planka, Kan or Focalboard, and Nextcloud Deck's "Activity Report" issue has essentially no traction. The people who want burndowns already left for Jira.

**The parking lot is the one planning primitive worth having, and columns nearly give it to you.** Pivotal Tracker's Icebox, Basecamp's "Not Now" column, and Things' Someday all solve the same thing: a place for ideas that isn't deletion and isn't clutter. Basecamp's framing — "things you decide not to prioritize, but don't want to lose" — is the whole pitch, and Things' rule is that inactive items "won't distract you from what's actionable right now." In Critical Path a column named Icebox already stores them; what's missing is the de-emphasis. The cheap, schema-shaped move is a second boolean on `board_column` alongside the existing `is_done` — collapsed/muted by default, excluded from the Ready view and from any future My Tasks — rather than a new object type. That's a small migration and it buys most of what Tracker's Icebox bought.

**Sprints are the clearest case of a tool imposing a process, and even the good implementation has a rough edge.** Linear's cycles are the version worth admiring: you configure a cadence once and it generates cycles forever, with no create-sprint ceremony, no naming, and automatic rollover — reviewers call it "sprint planning without the ceremony." Even so, the recurring complaint is boundary confusion, with tickets seeming to disappear and "confusion as to why they can't see tickets and where they've gone." Jira's version, which requires the snapshot-on-start plus explicit-disposition-on-complete pair to make its reports mean anything, draws "the backlogs, the sprints, the points — is pure bureaucracy." Pivotal Tracker's auto-velocity is the genuinely beautiful idea in this space (a freelancer could tell a client "unless something urgent crops up, it will be 18th-24th March"), but it needs points on every story and a weekly cadence — two obligations a Trello-shaped tool should never impose. Sprints are strongly wanted by teams that run sprints and irrelevant to everyone else; that asymmetry is what makes them wrong for a deliberately general tool.

### Skip these

- **Multi-view switcher as a framework** — a view framework is an invitation to fill it; ClickUp's 15 view types are why new users face "a sidebar with 15 navigation items" they can't parse.
- **Calendar view** — meaningless without a date field, and shipping the field to justify the view is the tail wagging the dog.
- **Timeline / Gantt / roadmap** — needs dates on *every* card plus range semantics; the obligation a simple board exists to avoid, and users report the cheap versions are "not at all usable."
- **Gallery as a separate view type** — a whole new surface for a payoff you get from card thumbnails on the board you already have.
- **Personal Inbox for unfiled capture** — you already have it: it's called a column. Global capture means tasks that belong to no project, which breaks the project-scoped access model at its root.
- **Today / My Day** — a personal-productivity product's core loop, not a team board's; needs dates and daily discipline the tool can't enforce.
- **Upcoming / next-7-days agenda** — pure date derivative; nothing to show without due dates.
- **Sprints / cycles / iterations** — imports an entire ceremony model that half your users actively fled; even Linear's excellent version confuses people at rollover boundaries.
- **Automatic velocity and auto-planned iterations** — beautiful, and it requires story points on everything plus a fixed weekly cadence; two impositions too many.
- **Burndown / velocity charts** — fragile enough that Atlassian maintains a library of "why is my burndown wrong" articles, and useless without estimates and sprints you shouldn't build.
- **Flow analytics (CFD, cycle-time scatter, Monte Carlo, flow efficiency)** — needs a status-history or daily-snapshot table you don't have; Monte Carlo and flow efficiency have essentially zero independent evidence of real-world use.
- **Widget dashboards over saved filters** — a second product bolted onto the first; every implementation ends in tier gates, empty gadgets and "reporting could be improved."
- **Portfolios / initiatives / roadmap rollup** — a tier above projects that exists to serve people who don't do the work; Asana's own community can't explain how it differs from Goals.
- **Goals / OKRs** — universally shipped, universally abandoned: "tasks and OKRs compete for attention, and tasks always win."
- **Hill charts** — clever enough that 37signals had to add a stale indicator because nobody could tell a stuck dot from a forgotten one; loved by Shape Up believers, invisible in ordinary reviews.
- **Workload / capacity planning** — requires estimates plus dates plus per-person capacity config; three data models for one manager screen.
- **Time-blocking / Planner** — the exact feature that made Trello's own users say the product had become "something like Notion or Jira"; also the most-abandoned feature in the personal-productivity category.
- **Guided daily planning ritual** — a wizard you can't skim is a strong opinion about how someone's whole day works; wrong altitude for a shared board.
- **Multiple boards over one task pool** — in Critical Path a project *is* the board; splitting them splits ownership and access too, for less benefit than saved views inside one project.
- **Curated "app" layer over the data (Interfaces)** — presupposes roles and per-field permissions the product deliberately doesn't have, and Airtable's own version drowns in caps and gaps.

## Search and filtering

Search is the category nobody praises and everybody notices the day a board outgrows one screen. Across all thirteen products studied, the complaint is almost never "add search" — it is "your search is bad": monday's own community carries a thread titled *"Search useless, since you can't actually go to anything in the results"*, and Confluence users report typing an exact title and getting *"tons of results, none of them even close."* Critical Path currently has no server-side search at all, but it does already have a working filter model hiding in the CLI (`cpath task list --label/--assignee/--ready/--blocked/--done/--search`, in `cli/src/commands/task.ts`), which means the highest-value item here is porting something that exists rather than inventing something new. The other durable lesson from the corpus: **filtering and searching are different jobs** — filtering keeps you in the board, search takes you out of it — and every tool that conflated them got worse.

| Feature | What it is | Who does it well | Why users like it | Critical Path | Fit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Global cross-project search | One box over task titles + descriptions across every accessible project; results open the task in its board | Trello (`/`), Linear, Jira; monday/Notion/Basecamp as cautionary tales | "Where did I put that thing" is the single most common lost-work moment; absence converts to abandonment fastest in OSS trackers | Missing | **Core** |
| In-board filter that narrows in place | Label / assignee / text / done predicates that dim or hide non-matching cards without leaving the board | Trello filter sidebar, Jira board filter bar, Kanboard | Keeps column layout and spatial memory intact, and you can still drag while filtered | Partial (CLI only) | **Core** |
| One-key quick filters ("my tasks", clear all) | Stackable named chips above the board; `q` = only my cards, `f` = open filter, `x` = clear | Trello (`q`/`x`), Jira Quick Filters ("Only My Issues") | Cheapest possible filtering UX; the shortcut people actually use daily | Missing | **Strong** |
| Filter state encoded in the URL | Filters live in the query string, so a filtered board is a link you can paste in chat | Jira, Linear (shipped Jul 2026), GitHub Projects | Bookmarkable + shareable; a poor man's saved view for a query-string parser's worth of work | Missing | **Strong** |
| Field operators with negation | A dozen prefixes over free text: `label:`, `assignee:me`, `is:done`, `is:ready`, `-` to negate, `no:assignee` | Jira JQL, Kanboard, GitHub Projects, Trello operators | JQL is the most-loved Jira feature and the one people miss after leaving; maps naturally onto a CLI-first product | Partial (CLI flags, no negation/empty) | **Strong** |
| Jump-to-anything palette | Type a project or task name from anywhere, Enter navigates — no results page | Things 3 (Type Travel), Linear (Cmd-K), TickTick | Speed, not sophistication, is what users mention; it's the cheapest form of search | Partial (CLI fuzzy refs) | Maybe |
| Saved / named filters | Filters saved as a first-class object, pinned to a sidebar or dashboard | Vikunja (saved filters as pseudo-projects), Todoist, Linear views, Asana reports | Turns "my open tasks" into a thing you save once instead of retype | Missing | Skip |
| Query language with nested AND/OR | A real grammar: boolean groups, parentheses, functions, precedence | Jira JQL, Todoist filters, Linear advanced builder | Genuine power-user delight for a minority | Missing | Skip |
| Multi-level stacked sorts | Prioritized sort rules applied to the board/list, overriding manual order | Notion, Airtable | Consistent ordering across views | Missing | **Anti-pattern** |

### Worth reading closely

**Filter and search are two products, not one.** Atlassian has a doc literally titled "Filtering vs searching," and the split is the most transferable idea in this whole set: filtering narrows cards *in place* so you keep board context and can still drag; search takes you out to a consolidated results panel. Trello's shortcuts encode the difference — `f` opens the filter, `x` clears it, `q` is one key for "only my cards," and all three show up in every shortcut cheatsheet people actually share. One trap from Trello's own docs: their keyword filter uses OR semantics across words, so "May 2009 Financial Planning" returns cards containing *any* of those words, which their docs concede surprises people. Pick AND across facets and AND across words, and say so in the placeholder text.

**Critical Path already built the filter — in the wrong client.** `cpath task list` already composes AND-ed predicates over column, label, assignee, done/not-done, ready/blocked (via `taskState` in `cli/src/board.ts`) and a case-insensitive title substring. That predicate set is designed, shipped and tested. Meanwhile `getBoardPayload` ships the entire board — tasks, labels, assignees, blockers — in a single response, so the web board filter is pure client-side work with literally zero network latency. Port the CLI's predicates to the frontend rather than designing a second filter vocabulary; the payoff is that a web chip bar and a CLI flag mean the same thing, and later an operator syntax can be the third skin on the same object.

**The empty-value and disappearing-card traps are where every clone gets burned.** Planka's issue tracker is a catalogue of exactly the wrong details: #1501 asks for a "No labels" option because there is *"no way to filter for cards that have no labels assigned or to effectively exclude cards with specific labels"*; #1342 is *"New cards disappear immediately if label filters are active"*; #808 asks that a card created under a filter inherit the filtered label. Trello's canonical shared trick is `-has:members` — "nobody picked this up." So: ship *Unassigned*, *No label* and *Not blocked* predicates on day one, not in v2. And note that Critical Path has realtime — a `task_created` event arriving into a filtered board is Planka #1342 waiting to happen, so decide explicitly whether an incoming non-matching task is dropped or shown with a "hidden by filter" affordance. Also render a visible chip bar: the classic support ticket in every tool is a user who forgot a filter was on and reports that their cards vanished.

**If you build search, build the boring one.** The negative evidence here is the strongest in the corpus. Basecamp: *"Search logic sucks... unless you type in the EXACT project id then it sits there."* Notion: *"if you need to search for specific data located in a database or several databases, you will have to navigate to each database to conduct your search manually."* monday: newly created items missing to index lag, misspellings returning nothing, and no way to clear the previous query. Marginalia_nu, who runs a search engine, diagnosed Confluence: *"Feels like they're just using a binary index and completely skipping relevance ranking."* The concrete build for this repo: one `GET /api/search` endpoint, a Postgres `tsvector` over title plus flattened description, scoped through the existing `accessibleProjectsFilter` in `src/services/authorization.ts` (so inaccessible projects stay invisible rather than 403-ing), `ts_rank` with title weighted above description, and a `pg_trgm` fallback for typos and prefixes. One wrinkle worth planning for: `task.description` is `jsonb` (restricted Tiptap), so you need a maintained flattened-text column or generated column feeding the index — do not try to rank over the JSON directly. The compensating advantage is that Critical Path's corpus is tiny: no comments, no checklists, no docs, no non-image attachments. The thing that makes Confluence's search bad is exactly the surface area this product doesn't have.

**Results must navigate, and freshness beats cleverness.** monday's most-ridiculed failure is a results row that doesn't take you to the item; users wrote an entire thread about it. Every search result in Critical Path should carry `project_id`, `column name` and enough context to deep-link straight into the board with the task open — and, because the board is realtime, the search index must reflect a task created ten seconds ago. Shortcut made a smart default change in Aug 2025: archived items are excluded from results unless explicitly asked for. Critical Path only archives whole projects, so the equivalent is one line — exclude tasks in archived projects by default, with an `is:archived` opt-in.

**Operators: yes, about a dozen, and only if they're discoverable.** JQL is the single most-loved Jira feature — a Capterra reviewer: *"the best feature is the JQL searching. When I learned how to use this, I was able to find very old Jiras by filtering"* — and Kanboard's little query language is what makes an otherwise plain tool powerful for solo users. But the consistent complaint about all of them is discoverability: nothing in Trello's UI teaches the syntax, so most users conclude Trello "can't find anything," and a r/scrum commenter on GitHub Projects said *"For technical people it's powerful, but it scares non-technical people."* Two mitigations are worth copying. Jira's basic/advanced toggle, where the dropdowns and the query string are two views of the same filter and the UI shows you the equivalent query — that's how most people learn JQL. And Linear's version, where typing text produces editable filter *chips* rather than an opaque query. For Critical Path the operator vocabulary should be exactly the CLI's flag set, so `cpath task list --help` doubles as the syntax documentation.

**URL state is the saved-filter feature, bought cheaply.** Wekan #319 ("Filtering should be made with the url to be able to bookmark them") and Nextcloud Deck #414 — which accumulated a *$167 community bounty* and 29 👍 before being closed — are the same request twice: users want to hand someone a link to a subset of the board. Vikunja's saved filters that appear as pseudo-projects are the most-loved variant of the heavier version, but saved filters are a new first-class object with naming, ownership, sharing, deletion and its own realtime events, and Trello never shipped board-level saved filters without nobody leaving over it. Encode filter state in the query string and let browser bookmarks be the saved filters.

**Know where the line is.** Todoist caps free users at three saved filters precisely because the query language is a power feature most people never touch; Things 3 refuses filter languages entirely and is loved anyway. An HN commenter framed it well: *"taskwarrior vs OmniFocus/Todoist/Things 3 is a similar vibe to vim vs IDE. Some people want a minimalist tool... some people want a discoverable batteries-included GUI. Both are fine."* Critical Path has already picked its side; nested boolean builders, saved reports and stacked sorts are what the other side buys.

### Skip these

- **Saved / named filters as first-class objects** — a whole new entity with naming, ownership, sharing, deletion and realtime events, to replace something a bookmarked URL already does; Asana users can't even search their own saved searches.
- **Query language with nested AND/OR groups** — a dozen operator prefixes cover the real demand; a boolean grammar with precedence is a second product that ships with a manual, and Vikunja #285 and Wekan #2044 show boolean semantics is exactly where users get burned.
- **Multi-level stacked sorts on the board** *(anti-pattern)* — in Notion, applying a sort disables manual card reordering; Critical Path's entire board model is float positions the user drags, so a sort control is a switch that silently turns off the product's primary interaction. (A `--sort` flag on `cpath task list --json` output is unrelated and harmless.)

## Collaboration and discussion

Critical Path is currently the only product in this comparison set where there is no way to say anything about a task. That is not a pre-Atlassian/post-Atlassian distinction: Trello shipped card comments, @mentions and a card activity feed years before the acquisition, and every deliberately-minimal open-source clone (Planka, Kan, Deck, Wekan, Kanboard) ships comments too — Kan lists "Comments: discuss and collaborate with your team" as one of only seven headline features. There is a second, sharper reason this section matters here: Critical Path has flat membership with no roles, so anyone with access can rename or delete anything. Every other shared-board product mitigates that with permissions or with an audit trail, and Critical Path currently has neither. The strongest evidence in the whole research set is Planka issue #296, which reads like a churn log ("this is the last missing feature before we can use Planka in production"; "Came here from trello becoming paid. This feature is a must have.") — one missing discussion feature blocking migrations people actively want to make.

### Worth reading closely

**Comments are the whole section.** The best articulation of why is the anti-Slack argument from HN: "You can't link to conversations. This means if you want to add context to a Jira ticket or in a code comment, you can't easily do so," and when discussion lives only in chat "all of that context disappears into the void." Build them flat and chronological, like Trello and Jira, not threaded. The ergonomic details users actually file issues about are small and specific: markdown/rich text (the Tiptap restricted schema already in `src/schemas/tiptap.ts` is the obvious reuse), editing and deleting your own comment, deep-linking to a single comment, and clipboard image paste — Nextcloud Deck #533 "Paste image on clipboard directly to comments like GitHub" has 32 👍 and 27 comments, and Critical Path already has the image upload pipeline that makes it cheap. Two traps. First, do *not* build comment edit history; Jira has had an open request for it since 2006 and a marketplace of paid apps filling the hole, and the honest answer for a small tool is an "edited" label and nothing more. Second, decide deliberately what project copy does with comments — Kanboard #4407 ("Duplicating task to another project removes comments and attachments") is filed as a bug, and Critical Path already ships project copy, so this is a decision that has to be made on day one rather than discovered later.

**@mention is the cheapest possible notification system, and Critical Path is unusually well placed to build it.** The product deliberately has no notifications of any kind, which normally makes "add mentions" a big commitment — except that a mention is the one notification model that needs no subscription table, no watchers, no digest and no preferences UI, because the sender explicitly opts exactly one named person in. Notion documents the behaviour people want verbatim: "To notify a particular teammate, just @-mention them in a comment, and they'll automatically receive a notification that they were mentioned." Critical Path already has an email service with a console/SES driver and post-commit send hooks, so mention → one email + one realtime event is close to the entire feature. Three design details, all evidenced: mentions must work in the **description**, not only comments (Deck has a separate 15-reaction request for exactly this; Asana only shipped description mentions in Nov 2025), the autocomplete must be scoped to people with project access (Notion warns that mentions can leak access to broadly-shared content — and Critical Path's whole authorization story is that inaccessible things 404), and above all: **notify once, do not subscribe forever.** Asana has a standing feedback thread titled "Team members should not be automatically added as collaborators after being mentioned in a task comment" — one mention to ask a quick question subscribes that person to a task permanently. Trello's version is better and worth copying: commenting on a card you don't follow *offers* a one-click Watch instead of assuming it.

**The activity log is not as free as it looks — build it from the transaction, not from the bus.** The research repeatedly claims that because every mutation already publishes a realtime event, an attributed history is nearly free. Having read the code, that's half true and the wrong half. The envelope in `src/services/realtime/bus.ts` is `{ type, project_id, data }` — there is no actor, `data` is the serialized row rather than an old→new diff, and `publishAfterCommit` runs as a post-commit hook, so an event can be lost if the process dies after commit. What you want is an append-only `task_activity` row written inside the same `transactionMiddleware` transaction as the mutation, with actor id and changed-field before/after, and the existing event published from it. The bus stays a delivery mechanism; the table becomes the record. Design details worth stealing: interleave activity with comments on the card back but keep Trello's "show details" toggle so the conversation reads as a conversation; record old→new like Jira's History tab and Airtable's revision feed, since "who moved this to Done and when" is the archetypal question; collapse aggressively ("Skyler moved 3 cards to Done"); and keep it forever rather than metering it — Airtable users have a whole culture of writing automations to keep their own change logs because retention expires, which is strong evidence that truncation is the thing people hate. The anti-pattern is documented too: Nextcloud Deck #2401 is literally "Activity notifications spamming user." Log everything, notify for nothing but mentions.

**Reactions: the constraint is the feature.** Both good implementations are explicit that reactions must not notify. Atlassian confirms "it's not possible to set something to receive emoji reactions in your mailbox," which is precisely why Trello reactions work as low-noise acknowledgement, and 37signals frames Basecamp Boosts as "a low ceremony way to share a little love, support what someone said, or say a little something without kicking off any noisy notifications" — with a hard 12-character cap so a Boost can never grow into a comment thread. Asana shipped a single heart and then spent years fielding "Feature Request: Emoji reactions instead of just 👍." So: ship reactions on comments (and probably on cards) with a small fixed picker rather than a full emoji keyboard, and wire them to zero notifications. This is a one-table, one-endpoint feature with disproportionate social value, and it's the thing that keeps a flat comment stream from filling with "+1" — a pattern open-source maintainers actively police, as when Deck's maintainer told a commenter "Please use the Github Reactions feature instead of commenting that [you] also need a specific feature."

**Flat beats threaded here, but steal Twist's Enter key.** Linear's threaded, resolvable comments with promote-to-sub-issue are genuinely admired in design teardowns, and Twist's titled-threads model is the most-loved thing about that product — HN's rtpg: "Doist's Twist thing is the 'real thing'. Forum threads/email convos for focused convos, stream-y chat otherwise. Mixing the two ends up being really messy." But both are answers to *volume*, and a three-to-ten-person board doesn't have volume; Capterra reviewers also report that Twist "takes time to adjust your brain" and felt "pretty confusing" at first, which is exactly the cost a simple tool shouldn't pay. The transferable idea is much cheaper: Twist makes Enter insert a newline rather than send, deliberately raising the cost of a reflexive one-word reply. As HN's tm-guimaraes put it, "the default settings of an app shapes a lot of the common behaviour." Pair that with reactions and you get the benefit of threading — fewer junk replies — without the data model.

**Image annotation is just comments with coordinates, and it's the one "advanced" item that fits this product.** Critical Path already stores per-task images behind unguessable capability URLs, which is the expensive half of proofing. Once comments exist, a pinned annotation is a comment row with an `x`/`y` and a resolved flag; ClickUp's version "impressed" the G2 reviewer, and the ability to "know exactly which part of an image needs edits and who gave that feedback" is Asana's own framing. The trap is scope: Asana's native proofing is thin enough that a whole ecosystem of paid replacements (PageProof as "official online proofing partner", Ziflow, Filestage, StreamWork, Artwork Flow) exists to sit on top of it, and Capterra reviewers name ClickUp's ceiling too ("lacks frame by frame review" for video). Half-built proofing invites a competitor into your own product. If you do this, do pins on images only — no versions, no compare, no review rounds, no approval states — and only after comments ship, never instead of them.

**Undo is the feature that matters most and costs most.** monday's community announcement reads "Made a mistake? Now you can finally UNDO!", which tells you both how wanted and how long-missing it was, and Asana users complain the activity log is inert: "there is currently no 'Undo' capability directly within the Activity Log, as it is read-only." General undo means an inverse for every mutation, and that's a large build. The 80% version for this product is much smaller and better targeted: because all FKs cascade and anyone with project access can delete anything, *deletion* is the only genuinely unrecoverable act on a Critical Path board. A soft-delete column plus a 7-day restore on tasks, columns and projects covers the actual disaster case that a no-roles sharing model invites, costs a fraction of general undo, and pairs naturally with the activity log ("Skyler deleted this column — restore").

### Skip these

- **Threaded, resolvable comment trees** — solves a message-volume problem a ten-person board doesn't have, doubles the comment data model and the UI, and Twist reviewers admit the model itself takes retraining.
- **Project status updates** — a reporting-upward ritual for stakeholders a small team doesn't have; Asana's AI auto-drafting of them is a confession that writing them is a chore, and a stale "On track" badge is worse than no badge.
- **Automatic recurring check-ins** — requires a scheduler and a notification channel to exist before it can work at all, to replace a standup that a three-person team either doesn't hold or holds in one message.
- **Docs / wiki next to the work** — reviewers say ClickUp's Docs "still feels like something added on top of a project management tool"; teams that already have Notion or Google Docs ignore bundled docs, and you'd inherit search, permissions and an editor you'd never win at.
- **Screen + audio recordings** — recording, storage, transcode and transcription for a nice-to-have with no evidence of retention value; a pasted Loom link in a comment gets 95% of it for zero code.
- **Whiteboards / mind maps** — a second product wearing the first one's login; the evidence is purchase-time enthusiasm ("unexpected capabilities... included at lower price points"), not usage.
- **Built-in team chat** — the documented failure mode: teams keep Slack because "Slack is always open, always notifying, independent of project management workflows," so you'd ship a chat nobody opens while the card comments you should have built stay empty.
- **Synced / reusable content blocks** — high complexity, high support burden, and a real footgun ("anyone who can edit a synced block can modify all instances across the workspace") on a product whose sharing model already has no roles.

## Notifications and awareness

Critical Path currently sends nothing: no bell, no email, no badge, no push. That is the right place to have started, because notification volume is the single most reliably hated subsystem in every product surveyed here — Jira's, Asana's, ClickUp's and Basecamp's notification systems are each their product's top complaint, and the first advice in every third-party guide is how to turn them off. The question is not whether to add notifications but which two or three, and for a Trello-before-Atlassian tool the answer is narrow: tell me when something is aimed at *me*, and put a dot on a board that moved while I was away. One structural advantage: `publishAfterCommit` already emits an event for every mutation, so an inbox is mostly a persistence-and-read-state layer over events that already exist — but note the envelope (`RealtimeEnvelope` in `/Users/skylerberg/Code/critical-path-api/src/services/realtime/bus.ts`) is `{ type, project_id, data }` with **no actor user id**, and nearly every good rule below depends on knowing who did the thing.

### Worth reading closely

**The one design law: notify only the directly involved, and never about your own actions.** Linear auto-subscribes you on exactly three acts — you created the issue, you were assigned it, you were @mentioned — and I found no complaints anywhere about Linear's notification volume, which is remarkable given how universal that complaint is elsewhere. Jira consultants tell administrators the same thing from the other direction: "a good Jira environment, except in rare cases, will only alert users who are directly involved in the issue." The self-notification bug is real and users notice it: Wekan's "Add option to receive notifications from other users only" has 11 👍, and Asana has a long-running forum thread about getting an Inbox notification when *you* complete a task assigned to *you*. Trello never notifies you about your own actions, including actions your own automations trigger — steal that verbatim. Concretely for this codebase: the realtime envelope carries no actor id today, so before any of this works you need to thread the acting `user_id` through `publishAfterCommit`. Do that even if you build nothing else this quarter; it's a small change that every later option depends on.

**The blocker-cleared notification is the one you should be excited about.** Every other alert in this document is a variation on "someone touched your card." Critical Path has a genuinely distinct trigger nobody else in the Trello-clone space has: a task's last blocker just moved to a done column, so the assignee's task is now *ready*. Linear treats blocking-relation changes as a first-class notification category; the CLI here already computes "ready" tasks. This is high-signal by construction (it fires rarely, only for the person who can now act) and it is the thing that would make someone describe the product to a colleague. If you ship exactly one notification, ship this one, not "task moved."

**The cheapest thing in this whole section is a dot, and it may be the best-value item on the list.** Wekan issue #1604 asks only for "a visual indicator for changes since last login" (6 👍) — no stream, no email, no settings. The Things 3 review quoted in the research makes the case for the inverse failure: "Beautiful. Expensive. Tricked me into thinking I had my life together. But I kept forgetting to check it." A per-user `project_last_seen(user_id, project_id, seen_at)` row plus a comparison against the newest change is a single small migration and one query joined into the existing projects list. Implementation caveat worth knowing up front: only `task` carries `updated_at` — `board_column`, `label` and `project` don't, and deletions aren't captured by a max-timestamp at all — so either accept "tasks changed" as the semantic (fine, and honest) or add a cheap `project.last_activity_at` bumped by the same place that publishes events.

**An inbox is cheap here and dangerous everywhere else.** Asana's Inbox is the single most-complained-about surface in this entire research set: consultants publish guides opening with "A lot of Asana users get overwhelmed by the inbox and end up missing important notifications and comments from their teammates," reviewers say "the overflow of notifications required for the system to work is crazy," and step one of every detox guide is "turn off email notifications" — a feature whose first-run experience is disabling it is a feature designed wrong. Twist shows the opposite failure: it's so non-intrusive that reviewers report "it's likely to miss a lot of messages especially if you're transiting from Slack." The narrow path between them is defined by what you *persist*, not by settings: write an inbox row only for direct-address events (you were assigned, you were added to a project, your task got unblocked), never for "a task in a project you belong to changed." If your inbox can ever have 200 items in it, you built Asana's.

**Email: two rules matter and the rest is noise.** First, don't email someone about something they already read in-app — Trello suppresses it, Notion only emails when the app isn't open, and this is described in the research as the most-copied piece of etiquette in the space with zero complaints attached. Second, cadence. Jira's failure here is legendary and worth quoting to yourself before writing any scheduler: "jira-1369 is a 20 year old bug of email notifications not being put into a digest and instead flooding users," one developer went "from 80-100 emails a day to 4-5" only by writing client-side filters, and a monday user reported 75 email reminders in a day. But note the escape: those products need digests because they generate hundreds of events. If you only email on direct address, you'll send a handful of messages per person per week, and a two-value setting (instant / off) is sufficient — build the digest only if volume ever proves you wrong. A digest also drags in a scheduler, per-user timezones and dedupe; Planka's community hit exactly this wall ("Planka has no cron support at this point"), and while a GKE CronJob is available here, it's still new infrastructure for a problem you may not have.

**Off switches beat scheduling, and every notification should carry its own.** Basecamp's three-tier scope (account → project → thread) exists because the flat version failed loudly, and reviewers *still* say "Every little thing seems to be sending me notifications which is extremely frustrating." An HN commenter's pushback on notification-overload complaints is instructive: "95% of people that say this don't mute channels (at all or enough)" — meaning a mute control that is hard to find is functionally absent. Two knobs cover almost all demand: mute this project, and globally "only tell me when someone addresses me directly." Put an unsubscribe link in the email itself, and auto-unsubscribe when a task is completed or you're unassigned — a Nextcloud Deck user specced this unprompted: "In notification, add button turn off notifications for this card… if a card is archived, notifications are turned off."

**Snooze is the sleeper.** Basecamp 5's "Bubble Up" and Linear's `H` (with natural-language input: "Jan 3 10am", "2d") both cost roughly one timestamp per user per item, and design teardowns single out Linear's natural-language snooze as the pattern to copy. What makes it valuable for a product with no dates is that it gives users most of the perceived benefit of reminders — "make this come back at me later" — without introducing due dates, a reminder entity, a scheduler with per-user timezones, or a second inbox. It's also the honest answer to anyone asking for personal reminders. Build it after the inbox, not before; snoozing needs somewhere to snooze *to*.

### Skip these

- **Quiet hours / "Work Can Wait" DND schedules** — it's a knob for a volume problem you shouldn't create in the first place; the research itself notes users drowning in notifications need scope control, not scheduling, and nobody on Capterra reports buying Basecamp for it.
- **Per-event × per-channel preference matrix** — Jira's most reliably hated subsystem, and the elaborateness of Asana's control panel is itself the evidence that its defaults failed; a preference matrix is a tax you pay for having generated too many notifications, so get the default volume right and you need three switches, not thirty.
- **Extra delivery channels and outbound gateways (push, Slack, Apprise, webhooks)** — each channel re-delivers the same message to another place the user has to mute separately, and a gateway drags in a whole integrations surface this product has deliberately avoided; hosted users want fewer channels, not more.
- **The personal-alarm half of reminders** (standalone "remind me at 4pm", DND-piercing urgent alarms) — that's Todoist/TickTick's product, not a shared board's; snooze covers the real need for one timestamp, and the notification-detox blogs list due-date and reminder alerts among the first things people switch off.

## Sharing, permissions and access

This is the category where simple project tools go to die: Jira's permission schemes, monday's column locks, Notion's six access levels. It is also where Critical Path has its one genuinely sharp edge — flat membership means anyone you share a project with can rename it, remove the other members, or delete it outright, and project deletion cascades every column, task, label and image with no undo, no per-task archive and no activity log to reconstruct from. The research is unusually clear about what the market actually wants versus what it tolerates: nobody in the corpus complained that a flat board let a teammate edit too much, but *the single most-upvoted feature request in the entire open-source kanban ecosystem* is a public read-only board link (Nextcloud Deck #14, 183 👍, roughly double its next issue). The right shape for this product is one destructive-action owner bit, one read-only level, one share link — and nothing else, ever.

### Worth reading closely

**The public link is the one thing to build, and the evidence isn't close.** Deck #14 at 183 👍 with a community bounty, Planka #602 as that project's #1 open issue, itsfoss's 2026 roundup still citing "public boards feature not yet implemented" as Planka's main criticism. The use cases people write down are exactly Critical Path's audience: "I just set up a Planka instance for my club because of the new restrictions Trello put on free accounts"; "I'd love to be able to make a board public but read only for an open source project of mine"; "being able to share specific boards as read-only with her clients to show the current work progress would be amazing." Note the workaround people invent when it's missing — a shared "Guest" pseudo-account with a password everyone knows — which is a security incident that the feature's absence causes. Note also the lost sale: an HN commenter walked away from Shortcut because "we weren't able to make our issues open to the public, which was a serious blocker."

**The implementation is small and Critical Path already owns the pattern.** The Deck maintainer's sketch is the whole design: "just add a field to the board table to have a public link token there. One link per board should be enough." A `share_token` column, a revoke/regenerate action, and an unauthenticated read path that reuses the existing bulk board payload. Task images already serve from unguessable capability URLs, so the token discipline exists in the codebase. Two non-negotiables. First, the token must be a separate slug, not the project id, so revocation is real. Second, read-only must be enforced in `authorization.ts` alongside the code that already answers 404 for inaccessible projects — not by hiding buttons. The failure mode is documented: a Nextcloud thread where read-only public links silently became writable ended with "anybody can upload and delete files in any public link to a groupfolder you have shared." The safest version also skips the realtime path entirely for anonymous viewers rather than trying to scope a `/ws` subscription to a token.

**Unlisted is not the same as public, and conflating them is how Trello leaked PII.** Trello's own docs describe public boards as "visible to anyone on the internet, but not indexed by search engines" — and yet in 2020 a researcher found large volumes of personal data exposed through public boards, because "public" is one dropdown away from "private." Ship one primitive: an unguessable, revocable, `noindex` link. Do not ship a visibility dropdown, do not ship a directory of public boards, and do not ship search-engine indexing. The default must be off and the toggle should say plainly what becomes visible (every card title, description and image on the board).

**The role ladder collapses to two rungs here, which is a gift.** Asana users describe the one scenario that justifies roles: "clients with commenter-only access while project managers have admin access and team members have editor access." Critical Path has no comments, so "commenter" has nothing to comment on — the four-level ladder collapses to *can edit* / *can view*. And because there is no workspace or org layer, membership is already project-scoped: a guest in Trello's sense is just a member here. That means the entire "guest access" feature that Basecamp puts on its front page and that Linear charges $16/user for reduces, for this product, to adding a `role` column with two values and a single `assertProjectWrite` check next to the existing `assertProjectAccess`. Linear's users are explicit about resenting the alternative: "guest accounts are priced as full users makes it too expensive for us."

**The owner bit is a blast-radius argument, not a governance argument.** The open-source research is honest that nobody complains about flat boards: "I found no thread where users of a flat-membership board tool complained that a teammate abused rename/delete rights." Fine — but the reason to restrict *delete* specifically is that in this codebase every FK is `ON DELETE CASCADE`, there is no undo, no per-task archive, and no activity history, so one mis-click by an invited collaborator is unrecoverable and unattributable. Restrict exactly two operations to the owner — delete the project and remove other members — and leave everything else flat. Do not add a settings screen. Every step past this is the Jira road: the Blind verdict is "you can't click a damn thing without a permissions issue," and Atlassian's own best-practice guidance reduces to "try to set up the scheme with only project roles in it," which is a confession that the full model is unusable.

**There is a real bug hiding in the current model: the creator is permanent.** The set-members endpoint strips `created_by` from the desired list, so an ordinary member can leave by omitting themselves, but the creator can neither leave nor be removed by anyone. `created_by` is nullable, so a deleted account leaves a project owned by nobody — and if you add an owner bit without an ownership transfer, that state becomes unfixable. Ship "leave project" as an explicit affordance and "transfer ownership" in the same change as the owner bit. Wekan users asked for a site-admin override for exactly this reason: "site admins to see all boards and change any board permissions," which is the classic problem of the person who created the board leaving the company.

**SSO is a self-hoster's request, and Critical Path is not self-hosted.** The huge vote counts — Wekan's LDAP issue at 80 👍 is the highest-voted issue in that repo's history, Plane's OIDC at 66 — come from people running the tool behind Authelia who don't want another password store. That population isn't the customer. What's left for a hosted product is "Sign in with Google," where the honest read of the evidence is that it's signup friction reduction rather than a purchase driver. Two lessons apply regardless. Never ship it free and then paywall it: Plane's "Move OIDC back to self-hosted OSS" issue (17 👍) generated more anger than never having shipped it. And if Google sign-in is ever added on top of existing password accounts, link by verified email in place — the Atlassian forced-relink is the cautionary tale of the decade, with users reporting "two separate Trello accounts that use the same email address" and having to "request to share" their own boards back. Identity migrations that break access to existing data cost more trust than any feature buys.

**Email verification is the cheapest item on this list and the one with a real support cost today.** Password reset is a stateless HMAC link mailed to whatever address was typed at signup. If that address has a typo, the account is unrecoverable the moment the 30-day session expires — and by then it holds someone's actual board. The email pipeline already exists for reset and feedback; this is a token, a route and a flag. It also makes "add member by exact email" mean something, since the address you're granting access to has been proven to belong to that person.

### Skip these

- **Search-indexable public boards** — Trello's own dropdown put real PII into Google's index; ship the unlisted `noindex` link instead and gain the same benefit with none of the exposure.
- **Per-item client visibility (Basecamp client mode)** — a visibility boolean on every card turns every write path into a permission check and every board read into a filtered view, for a workflow that only makes sense in a tool with comments and posts, which this isn't.
- **Permission schemes, column locks, field-level and issue-level security** — this is the exact machinery that made Jira the thing users flee ("you can't click a damn thing without a permissions issue"), and every rule added becomes a support request only the owner can resolve.
- **Admin audit log** — pure compliance overhead with no small-team demand in the entire corpus; the audit trail people actually want is per-task history ("who moved this card"), which is a different feature in a different section.

## Automation, templates and integrations

This is the category where every simple tool goes to die, and also where Critical Path is already 80% of the way to the only version of it worth shipping. The pattern across all thirteen products is consistent: users love a *small fixed set* of automations and a *clean machine-readable surface*, and they resent rule builders, metered "command runs", template galleries and AI agents — which is exactly the accretion that made Trello feel bloated after 2018. Critical Path already has a full REST API, an OpenAPI spec, a generated client, a real CLI with `--json`, and a post-commit event bus (`publishAfterCommit`) that fires `task_created` / `task_updated` / `column_*` / `label_*` / `project_*` on every mutation. That means the highest-value items here (webhooks, tokens, agent surface) are adapters over machinery that already exists, and the honest answer to "will you integrate with X?" is "here's the webhook."

### Worth reading closely

**Webhooks are the cheapest "yes" you will ever ship, and they are already 90% built.** `publishAfterCommit` runs as a post-commit hook and emits precisely the event stream a webhook subscriber wants — nothing is published on rollback, and access is already scoped per project. A webhook is a second subscriber on that bus plus a `project_webhook` table, an HMAC signature, retry with backoff, and a delivery log. The strategic value is that it lets you decline every integration request without saying no: users route around missing automation rather than waiting (a Planka user published a third-party `planka_automations` repo purely because recurring cards didn't exist). The one quality detail that decides whether anyone trusts it is payload stability — Kan.bn's issue #457 is a user discovering that `data.list.id` "returns different values for the same list across events", which destroyed confidence in the whole integration. Emit stable UUIDs, keep the envelope identical across event types, and version it.

**Automation should be a settings page, not a product.** Butler is the most-praised advanced Trello feature and simultaneously the exhibit for post-acquisition rot — not because rules are bad but because Atlassian metered them (250 command runs/month on Free; an Atlassian community thread is titled "'Unlimited' really means 10,000 or fewer operations per month"). Jira, Asana, monday and ClickUp all repeated the same move, and Asana's forum has a multi-page thread calling the 20-rules-per-project cap "completely limiting". Meanwhile GitHub Projects ships *four* toggles and an HN commenter says its automation "surpasses Jira's". The lesson for Critical Path is to pick three or four behaviours and hardcode them: complete a task when it's dropped into an `is_done` column; assign the mover; and — the one nobody else can offer — when a task completes, surface every task it was blocking as newly ready. That last one is the namesake feature doing work on its own, and it costs a query you already have in `src/services/dependencies.ts`. Do *not* build a rule editor; the moment you do, you also own rule ordering, an execution log, and "why didn't my rule fire" support tickets. ClickUp reviewers report "automations breaking frequently, which is a huge problem", and Notion's relations essay puts it bluntly: "every automation you build is a future debugging session waiting to happen. Automated systems fail silently."

**Recurrence and the .ics feed are the same decision wearing two hats, and it isn't made in this section.** Both are dead until tasks have dates. But note how loud the demand is: Deck #480 "Recurring cards" is its highest-voted non-sharing request at 96 👍 with users writing the spec themselves ("mark a card as done, and for it to then automatically reappear with the new due date… So there'd always just be one card"), and Planka's ixvari says flatly "+1, If this feature was in, I'd switch over to it right now." Calendar interop is even higher in aggregate — 72 👍 plus the largest bounty in the Deck repo ($185) for read CalDAV, 69 👍 for write, 35 on Focalboard, 27 on Planka, 20 on Wekan. If dates ship, a read-only `.ics` URL is the single highest value-per-line-of-code item in this entire catalogue: one endpoint, no new view, no new mental model, and it satisfies most of the "calendar view" demand outright. Full CalDAV write is the tarpit — Vikunja has it and still fields iOS incompatibility bugs.

**Get recurrence semantics right or don't ship it.** ClickUp is the cautionary tale and the quotes are brutal: "I have spent hours on CU recurring… and still don't have a grasp on how it works"; "I have given up on recurring tasks. I do not have the time to waste"; "It shouldn't be this difficult." Todoist's model is the one to copy — `every 3 months` recurs from the scheduled date, `every! 3 months` from the completion date, and overdue recurrences skip forward to the next future occurrence instead of generating a pile. Basecamp's data model is the other half worth stealing: a hidden primary to-do owns the schedule and spawns concrete instances, so editing the series and editing one occurrence are different operations. That split is far harder to retrofit than to design in. A weak recurrence engine earns complaints, not credit.

**Duplication beats templates, and the evidence for that is unusually clean.** Critical Path deliberately dropped `is_template`, and every product in this corpus argues that was right. Jira admins report that templates are the main driver of scheme sprawl; Asana's top community complaint about templates is a design bug ("template tasks appearing in My Tasks" — phantom work polluting everyone's list); Notion workspaces decay into "competing templates"; and the observed pattern across OSS clones is a substitution effect — teams that get board-copy stop asking for templates. Meanwhile plain duplication is heavily requested and nearly free: Deck's clone-card issue drew 57 👍 and 44 comments, clone-board attracted a $150 bounty, Planka has three separate duplication requests. Critical Path already has `projectCopy.ts`; the missing and more frequently exercised half is **duplicate this task**, with a target column and checkboxes for what carries over. Build that, and revisit card templates only if people keep asking after they have it.

**Intake is where a simple tool can beat the big ones, but only paired with a destination.** Asana's forms get real, non-PM enthusiasm precisely because of two design choices: the submitter needs no account, and rules route the submission so nobody triages by hand. Email-to-board earns the same affection for the same reason — Trello's per-*list* address is better than Asana's per-*user* address because it addresses the destination, not the person (Asana's own documented limitation: "the new task lands in My Tasks unassigned to a project"). Linear's insight is that all intake funnels into Triage rather than the backlog, which is what makes wide-open intake safe. For Critical Path both are Maybe rather than Strong, for different reasons: a form is a whole unauthenticated public surface plus spam handling, and email-in means owning an inbound MX pipeline (the outbound SES driver in `src/services/email` gives you nothing here). If you want one, the form is the better bet — it's a single POST that creates a task in one named column, with no rich-text parsing, no attachment size cliff, and no "10 MB email silently dropped" support thread.

**Ship the machine surface, not the machine.** The durable 2026 demand is not AI *in* the product — it's a clean API/CLI/MCP surface so users can point their own agent at it, and Critical Path already has more of this than Basecamp did until 2026. The named gaps in the corpus are specific and small: batch/bulk creation, idempotent operations, and a `schema` command for agent discovery (one HN author built "an AI-native CLI for Todoist" that "treats both humans and AI agents as first-class users"). Two things stand between `cpath` and being genuinely agent-grade: a personal access token that isn't a 30-day session token, and published docs for the OpenAPI spec you already dump. Contrast with the alternative: Height bet the entire company on autonomous AI project management, was widely called "one of the most beautiful apps out there", raised $18.3M, and shut down in March 2025 because the pitch "could not move the needle." And from the HN thread on Pivotal Tracker successors: "As soon as the AI features start showing up... it's time to jump ship."

**One integration, if any, and it should be Slack unfurl.** Asana has 100+ integrations and the only one anyone praises unprompted is GitHub ("Asana's GitHub integration is superb"); the other 98 are a marketing number. Linear's Slack app is loved for unfurling and for creating an issue with the originating conversation attached — the low-noise half. The high-noise half is the channel firehose, and users are explicit about it: "If Slack admins go crazy on integrations, you can still get a lot of low-value notifications." Ship unfurl (title, assignees, column, blocked-by — a nice showcase for dependencies), make any feed opt-in and per-event-type. On git integration, note the prerequisite nobody mentions: branch-name linking needs a short human-readable task key, which Critical Path doesn't have. Given a key plus the existing CLI, a documented `post-commit` hook recipe gets you 80% of Shortcut's integration for zero server code — and the demand for deep git sync concentrates in engineering trackers (Plane, Huly), not in general Trello-likes like Planka or Deck where it barely appears.

### Skip these

- **Trigger → condition → action rule builder** — the single highest bloat vector here: a rule editor, an execution log, rule-ordering semantics, and silent-failure support load, all to replace three hardcoded behaviours. Every vendor that built one ended up metering it, and the metering is what users call bait-and-switch.
- **Public template gallery / duplicate-by-link** — an acquisition channel for VC-funded products, not a feature for users. It requires public sharing (which the product doesn't have) and produces "competing templates" decay for the people who adopt it.
- **Third-party extension marketplace (Power-Ups / Packs)** — a marketplace is a second product with review, versioning, sandboxing and mobile-parity obligations. Webhooks plus the public API is the honest version of this promise for a solo-maintained tool.
- **Auto-add cards from a saved filter** — inverts the board into a standing query; Critical Path's hand-ordered columns with float positions are deliberately the opposite design, and simpler.
- **SLAs / automatic deadline policies** — brings working-week settings, risk bands and rule ordering to a tool with no dates and no notifications. Zero user sentiment behind it in any source; it exists because Linear started selling to support teams.
- **Weighted customer requests on tasks** — turns a board into a product-management platform. The transferable idea (count and attribute feedback rather than just emailing it) is worth a note, not a schema.
- **In-product AI assistant / autonomous agents** — the clearest anti-recommendation in the whole catalogue. Trello forum users describe what they valued as "Trello's straightforward, uncluttered approach... without forced features like AI integration"; Linear's HN thread reads "Linear was really, incredibly fast... Now it's bloated"; Height died on this exact bet. Expose the API and MCP instead and let users bring their own model.
- **Auto-categorising list (grocery mode)** — genuinely delightful, and genuinely a different product. Its own advocate concedes it's "more trouble than they're worth" without shared household lists and store-section semantics.

## Platform, clients and data

For a tool whose entire pitch is "Trello before it got bloated", this category *is* the product. Nobody switches trackers for a feature list; they switch because the incumbent got slow, got expensive, or got in the way — "it's so bloated and slow I find myself spending half the time waiting" is the single most repeated sentence about post-Atlassian Trello, and 89% of Jira-to-Linear switchers named speed as their #1 reason. Meanwhile the things that decide whether a switch is even *possible* are unglamorous plumbing: can I get my Trello boards in, can I get my data back out, does it work on my phone, will the free tier be cut out from under me. Critical Path already owns the two hardest foundations (realtime, and client-supplied ids that make optimistic writes correct rather than hopeful); most of what follows is small, cheap, and disproportionately load-bearing.

### Worth reading closely

**Speed is the positioning, and the WebSocket is the specific threat to it.** The evidence here is unusually blunt: dekerta on Trello — "it's so bloated and slow I find myself spending half the time waiting"; lmm — "Atlassian bought it and started making it bloated and slow"; on Jira — "It's hard to name a single action I can take in JIRA which does not feel unacceptably slow" and "Dragging an issue from one column to another… greys out the board, rendering it unreadable and unusable for 5-ish seconds"; ClickUp's own feedback board has 1.1k voters on a thread containing "I've seen turtles accomplish a full day's workout faster than it takes to fully choose an emoji". jayparth's framing is the important one: "People complain about Jira being slow… No one has claimed that all issue trackers are slow" — users understand slowness as a *choice*. The trap that is specific to this architecture: a naive client applies its own broadcast event back onto local state, producing a visible re-render or card re-order a beat after the user's own drag. Client-supplied ids make self-originated events identifiable — the client must drop or idempotently merge its own echo, and never re-sort from a server event that only confirms what it already did. Also: never grey out the board during a mutation, and never full-page-navigate between board and task detail.

**What makes Trello's keyboard model good is the selection model, not the key list.** Hover *or* keyboard focus designates a target, then unmodified single keys act on it — no focus ceremony, no chords, identical on Mac and Windows. An HN commenter defending Trello against clones cited "excellent keyboard navigation" and noted most alternatives "lack this attention to detail." Linear's addition worth stealing is that the command palette prints each command's shortcut inline, so the palette teaches the shortcut system as a side effect of being used. Three traps, all documented: (1) unmodified global keys fire when focus is on the page — "If it's not an input field you've focused but the page itself, you might assign the ticket to yourself" — so destructive and assignment keys need a confirm or a modifier; (2) ClickUp ships shortcuts *disabled by default*, which is self-defeating; (3) GitHub Projects bound item creation to Enter alone and broke card creation on mobile keyboards entirely, with the thread still unanswered years later — never let a keyboard chord be the only path to an action. And judge the palette on total keystrokes to finish a real task, not on existing: knes's complaint about Linear was that despite the palette you "still need too many clicks/keyboard shortcuts for simple tasks like adding 2+ issues."

**A Trello importer is the single highest-leverage thing on this list, and the failure modes are already written down for you.** Trello *cannot re-import its own JSON export* — Atlassian says so explicitly — so this is a wedge, not parity. Demand is stated as a switching cost, not a feature: bryanrasmussen, "Anyone know a good simple trello competitor that I can import my trello boards to really quick"; tomjuggler settled on Wekan because "you import Trello boards into, and self host." The open-source bug trackers are effectively a spec for what a good importer handles: Planka #598 (due dates ignored), #599 (members ignored), #789 (comments dropped), #1343 (large files fail); Deck #5137 (long card names), #5353 (null label colours), #5572 (API card import fails); Vikunja crashes on nil pointers and imports archived boards you can't then touch. A half-working importer generates more support load than none. Critical Path's model has *no* due dates, checklists or comments, which makes an explicit mapping preview mandatory rather than optional — say up front "checklists become a list in the description; due dates and comments cannot be imported because we don't have them." barnum's complaint is exactly what happens otherwise: "you can import Trello projects but it's a one-way import, not that integrated." Stream large files, map members to accounts by email with an unmatched-members report, and preserve card order.

**Export is a trust artifact and you are two-thirds of the way there.** The emotional core is xenithorb on Nextcloud Deck's export request (79 reactions, a $30 bounty): "I'm concerned about putting a lot of effort into setting up and detailing many boards for myself, and then somehow losing all of it… I'd like to not only be able to export it in an importable format, but in some kind of flat-file way too — perhaps a spreadsheet — so that I know my work and time I put into this is safe." Trello gates CSV behind Premium, truncates JSON to the 1000 most recent actions, and excludes comments — users read that as hostage-taking, and a free complete export buys trust cheaply against it. The REST API plus `--json` satisfies developers; it does not satisfy the person who wants a download button. Two design points: make the JSON export the *same format the importer reads* (an export that can't be re-imported is a checkbox, not a feature — you're building the parser anyway), and note karlicoss's objection that one-shot exports rot — "your export stales, it's just so frustrating" — which the public API already answers for anyone who wants continuous backup.

**Mobile: the responsive web board is the requirement, native apps are the aspiration, and the gesture model is where you can actually beat Trello.** The hard blocker quote is unambiguous — "I use the trello app on my phone a lot while I commute… I don't think I can switch to another tool until it has a decent (Android) app" — but the same threads say Trello's own card-moving "is really hard to use and lags a lot," and the open-source field is a graveyard: Focalboard's #1 open issue is mobile apps (75 reactions), Wekan #953 is literally titled "Hard (sometimes impossible) to use Wekan on mobile," Planka #19 has been open since August 2020 with 4jag reporting years later that "Planka is still completely unresponsive." The mechanics everyone gets wrong: long-press to pick up (not desktop drag emulation), auto-scroll at screen edges, horizontal column paging with vertical card scroll, card detail as a full-screen sheet rather than a cramped modal, 44px targets, and — critically — a non-drag "Move to…" fallback so a card can always be moved without dragging at all. That last one is the same command the accessibility story needs and the same one the CLI already exposes: build move as a first-class verb and it pays out three times. On scope, every large product converged on the same lesson from the wrong direction: Asana users report it is "difficult to get anything done beyond quickly checking task status," ClickUp attempted parity and produced crashes and overwrite conflicts. Optimise mobile for capture and glance. Jira supplies the counter-caution: narrowing is fine, but don't narrow past a whole role's primary action ("the app's restriction to view only your issues makes it useless… for product owners who are doing the assigning").

**Offline: ship the cheap half and don't say "local-first".** The gap between the two things hiding under this label is enormous, and Linear — the poster child — is where the warnings come from: "I have mysteriously lost comments/descriptions"; "My Linear client gets stuck syncing… completely blocking all interaction"; "repeated writes sometimes overwrite themselves"; "the app silently discarded one of their inputs because of a conflict"; and, fatally for the marketing, "If I open the Mac app when offline I just get a blank window… no access to any of my data." A local-first sync engine does not automatically buy you offline, and it definitely buys you a new class of silent data-loss bug. Notion's belated offline mode is the other cautionary shape — scoped per page, 50-row database cap, web excluded — and "partial offline that silently truncates data is its own complaint generator." The tractable version here is a service-worker cached board read plus an optimistic mutation queue keyed on client-supplied ids, with reconnect resolved by re-fetching the board payload rather than replaying float positions (position conflicts are the genuinely hard part and are not worth solving). One commenter draws the line correctly: "a local-first app is designed to function completely offline for an indefinite period. The local experience is the foundation, not a fallback" — so don't claim it.

**Accessibility has an unusually cheap floor and one product-specific hazard.** dexwiz's estimate is that sensible tab order, form labels and keyboard navigation get you "80% accessibility compliance," and blind users are explicit that standard HTML used in the intended way — links, buttons, headings, lists — simply works. The hazard is that a board mutating from WebSocket events is precisely the case screen readers handle worst: elements that "frequently change/disappear/reappear" are "really annoying" because text-to-speech can't scan the DOM the way a sighted user scans a screen. So realtime announcements need a live region that is throttled, coalesced ("3 cards moved") and mutable. On drag-and-drop, JMTQp8lwXL states the standard failure — "it works with a mouse, but not keyboard navigation" — and jscholes is scathing about implementations that merely claim support: react-beautiful-dnd's "drag and drop sample doesn't 'work well with screen readers'. It's overly verbose." DGCA's "Pick n Plop" (select the item, view placement options, choose one) is the pattern to copy because it is accessible by construction rather than by retrofit.

**Trust posture — pricing, status page, export, self-host — is one bundle, and it is the cheapest differentiator in this whole document.** Trello's 2022 free-plan board cap is named, by name, as the moment people started evaluating alternatives: ScanuNicco, "I just set up a Planka instance for my club because of the new restrictions Trello put on free accounts"; ajimix, "Came here from trello becoming paid." Incumbent users are openly anxious — 20k: "Trello is still my goto for everything. I sincerely hope that it doesn't get enshittified." monday's seat-bucket rule earned the nickname "ghost seat tax" ("a team of six pays for ten seats") and a 2.7/5 Trustpilot across 3,300+ reviews; bombcar's structural complaint is about cliffs — "go from 3 to 4 or otherwise pass some arbitrary limit and you go from $free/month to $300/mo." A written, dated promise that existing accounts never lose limits they already have costs nothing and is directly responsive to the thing that created your addressable market. Pair it with a public status page that breaks out the WebSocket as its own component (a realtime product can have healthy REST and dead sockets, and users describe outages by exactly which operations broke — "a major multi-day outage that prevented commenting on or moving cards, so I would be hard pressed to recommend it to anyone"), and with a compose file, since the friction complaints in self-hosting threads are always packaging rather than features: "This would be easier to test out if I could just run a docker image."

### Skip these

- **Full local-first sync engine** — Linear's own users report lost comments, stuck sync, and silently discarded edits; you'd trade a working realtime board for a distributed-systems maintenance burden and a new failure mode users experience as data loss.
- **Desktop app with pinned tabs** — a second client to build, sign, notarise and update forever, delivering what browser tabs plus deep links already deliver; Linear's own desktop app shows a blank window offline.
- **Voice/AI dictation capture** — network-only, quota-metered, and a parser to maintain; it's Todoist's killer feature because Todoist is a capture product, whereas a board tool's capture path is one text field.
- **Per-user or per-workspace feature toggles (and a second "simple mode" project type)** — configurability is what bloat looks like when you're too polite to say no; ClickUp shipped ClickApps and still reads as "a confusing mess," and Jira had to build a second product inside itself to escape its own options. Defaults are the product.
- **SQLite or any second database backend** — doubles the migration and query surface permanently to save self-hosters one container, on a codebase whose whole speed story rests on Postgres-specific bulk queries.

## Delight, personalisation and extras

This is the one category where "yes" is often the right answer for a deliberately small tool, because the best things in it add no field to a card and no concept to learn — pre-Atlassian Trello won partly on how it *felt*, not on what it tracked. It is also the category with the sharpest split: half of it is free feel (a completion animation, a board colour, a published stance) and half is personal-productivity machinery (karma, streaks, habit chains, timers, dashboards) that quietly converts a shared board into a self-measurement device. The second half is doubly wrong here, because Critical Path has no dates on tasks at all — scoring "on time" would require inventing the date model the product deliberately omits. Judge each item by whether it changes the schema: the ones that don't are cheap differentiators, the ones that do are the start of the bloat you're reacting against.

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A published method page | A short public doc naming what the tool refuses to do and the one way it expects you to work | Linear (`linear.app/method`), Basecamp (Shape Up) | Makes opinionation read as confidence, not limitation; "reduces decision fatigue and accelerates team onboarding" | Missing | Core |
| Completion celebrations | Brief, random, non-blocking animation when a task hits done, with an off switch | Asana (creature on a rainbow trail), Linear (subtle completion animation) | "Users immediately fell in love"; Asana claims 19% more tasks completed per session with celebrations on | Missing | Strong |

### Worth reading closely

**The method page is the highest-leverage item on this list, and it is not software.** Linear's Method is a web page, and analyses credit it as the reason Linear's narrowness reads as taste: "instead of trying to support every possible workflow, it narrowed the surface area of choice. Defaults are strong. Options are limited." For Critical Path this is load-bearing rather than decorative, because the product is *defined* by its absence list — no due dates, no custom fields, no roles, no priority. With no page explaining that, every one of those reads to a visitor as unfinished; with one, they read as the product. And you have a real method to publish rather than a borrowed one: dependencies instead of priority, and "ready" (unblocked and unfinished) as the answer to *what do I work on next* — that is an actual methodological claim, it is the namesake, and no competitor makes it.

**The trap in the method page is copying Linear's shape instead of its move.** Linear's Method prescribes two-week cycles, project specs, single owners, twelve practices — process rituals the product then enforces, which produces the standing complaint: "Linear is opinionated by design, and those opinions are shaped by how Silicon Valley engineering teams work. If your team does not follow a triage-to-backlog-to-cycle workflow, Linear will fight you rather than adapt." A Trello-like tool has no business prescribing ceremonies. Write the shorter document: what the tool refuses to add and why, plus one practice (pull the next unblocked task). It doubles as a roadmap-defence artifact you can point at every time someone asks for custom fields.

**Celebrations are the cheapest differentiator available to you, and the mechanic is rarity.** Asana's creature started as an engineer's April Fool's joke and became the single most affectionately discussed thing in this whole research set — the top follow-up request is for *more* creatures and customisable ones, which is what "loved" looks like. It works because it is occasional and random rather than every time (so it never becomes a modal you learn to dismiss), brief, non-blocking, and switchable off. Treat the "19% more tasks per session" number as Asana's own marketing, but the community sentiment is unusually one-directional: "the Asana community forum is filled with praise for all of these bubbly characters."

**Concretely, celebrations cost Critical Path almost nothing on the backend.** Columns already carry `is_done` and `task_updated` already broadcasts the full board task shape, so the client can fire on the transition into a done column with no new endpoint and no migration. Two decisions worth making deliberately: whether a teammate's completion celebrates on *your* screen — the realtime event makes it possible and a shared moment is the stronger version, but it is also the version that gets irritating first on a busy board, so gate it to the actor initially — and where the off switch lives. There is no user-settings table today; keep the preference in client storage rather than growing a settings column, a settings endpoint and a settings screen for one boolean.

**Backgrounds are worth having for navigation, not decoration — take the cheap half.** The genuinely useful finding from Trello is that long-time users recognise boards by background colour before they read the name; it is wayfinding disguised as prettiness. A per-project colour — one nullable column, a swatch in the project list, no upload pipeline — captures nearly all of that benefit. The full version pulls in cropping, storage and curation, and it carries Trello's own cautionary tale: custom background upload was a Gold/Business Class upsell, still cited by reviewers as the original example of small delights being paywalled. Your existing avatar and task-image pipeline (upload, WebP normalisation, storage keys) makes going further tempting precisely because it is easy; resist past the colour. Stickers are the counter-example to celebrations — I found promotional coverage and no organic user praise, and per-card visual state is the first step onto the card-covers road that ends in the modern Trello card.

**Focus mode's unlock is the removal of the list, not the timer.** That distinction is why I split it from the gamification bucket: Marvin's Super Focus Mode simply shows one task, Sunsama's mutes other apps, and the demand is real enough that someone shipped an entire app (Underload) to do nothing but show Apple Reminders one item at a time — "I always hate opening Reminders to look at an endless list of tasks. There's just no linear path forward." Critical Path is closer to this than anything else in the section because it already has the hard part: `cpath ready` computes unblocked-and-unfinished client-side from the existing board payload, so the web equivalent is a filter and a big card, with no schema change and no endpoint. One guardrail from Sunsama's founder, who refuses algorithmic task selection because "you will make a better decision about what to work on than an algorithm": show ready tasks as a shortlist to choose from, never as a machine-picked "your next task."

**The gamification family is one entry because it is one design move: a second scoreboard that measures the user instead of the work.** The clearest evidence against it is inside Todoist's own product — Karma ships with both a global off switch *and* a vacation mode, two separate escape hatches built to defuse the overdue penalty. A feature that needs two pressure-release valves is a feature fighting a meaningful slice of its users. Note also what Karma actually rewards: points for using labels, recurring dates and reminders, i.e. the scoring partly exists to drive feature adoption, which is exactly the incentive a deliberately small tool should never build. The habit-tracker half fares no better in the wild — "I've tried Todoist, Notion, Pomodoro, habit trackers, morning routines, 'don't break the chain,' Beeminder... All abandoned within weeks" is a recurring theme, and the thing that actually worked for that person was another human asking. The stats-dashboard half is milder, merely off-mission rather than harmful, but I found no user testimony defending Todoist's charts specifically; it is the sort of feature that wins reviews and loses attention.

### Skip these

- **Karma points, levels and streaks** — would require inventing the due-date model Critical Path deliberately lacks just to compute "on time", and Todoist needs two separate off switches to contain the damage its version does.
- **Habit tracking as a separate object type** — a whole second object with its own recurrence and check-in rules, serving a single-player use case a shared board doesn't have, with an unusually well-documented abandonment rate.
- **Per-task pomodoro timers and productivity dashboards** — turn a team board into a self-surveillance surface, drag in time-tracking data nobody asked for, and attract far more review praise than actual use.
- **GIPHY stickers and any per-card visual customisation** — no organic user praise even for Trello's own version, and card-level decoration is the first step toward the bloated card UI this product exists to reject.
- **Custom uploaded board backgrounds (as opposed to a colour)** — real cropping/storage/curation cost for the decorative part of a benefit that is actually about wayfinding, and Trello's paywalling of exactly this is still held against it.

## Second-pass findings

The first pass studied *the board*, and studied it close to exhaustively. What it missed is everything that surrounds the board, and the reason is structural: all ten researchers picked general-purpose GUI SaaS (or GUI clones of it), so the corpus is organised around cards, columns and views. Four things fall outside that frame and therefore appear nowhere in 400 rows — (a) the **identity lifecycle**: how a collaborator who has no account gets *in*, how a user gets *out*, and who inherits a project when the person who made it leaves; (b) **multi-user semantics**: "realtime sync" appears five times, presence and edit-conflict zero times, even though Critical Path stores a rich-text description two people can silently clobber; (c) the **CLI as a product**, not as a transport for board features — no researcher studied a CLI-native tracker, so completion, stdin, streaming and exit codes never came up; and (d) **vertical specialisation** — nobody looked at a tool built for one industry, including the two built for the author's own. A secondary cause compounds all four: every researcher hit the same blocked Reddit and the same exhausted search budget, so the sentiment base is Hacker News and GitHub issues — self-hosters and developers, precisely the audience least likely to complain about invite friction, colour-blind labels or "how do I hand this board to someone else". The non-software teams in the brief (clubs, nonprofits, freelancers with clients, events) are the least-served part of the research, and they are exactly who the rows below are about.

### Worth reading closely

**Invitations are the adoption blocker, and everything else on this list is downstream of them.** Today `POST /api/projects/:id/members/by-email` 404s on an address that has no account, which means Critical Path cannot do the single most common thing a person does with a project tool: hand a half-built board to someone who has never heard of it. The workaround is a two-step out-of-band ritual — message them, wait for them to sign up, then add them — and it fails silently in the most likely case, where you type the address they gave you and they sign up with a different one. Two design constraints fall out of the research and both need deciding *before* the invite table is written. First, membership must stop being "a row keyed by exact email match" and become "a row keyed by a user id, reachable by any address that user has proven", or invite-claim can never work. Second, WorldMaker's point on HN is the requirement: do not "assume that the address an invite is delivered to is the primary address for that person nor the intended address for their account with you." Email verification (Strong, above) is the same project seen from another angle — it is what makes "proven address" mean anything, and without it an unverified typo'd address is simultaneously a permanent lockout (reset-by-email is the only recovery path) and a misdelivery vector for the invite itself. This cluster is one coherent piece of work, roughly two weeks, and it converts the product from "a board for people who already use it" into "a board you can start a team on".

**The CLI is the differentiator and it is currently three cheap features short of being loved.** No researcher studied a CLI-native tracker, so the corpus has nothing on this — but the reference designs are unambiguous and the gaps are days of work, not months. `gh` ships completion for bash, zsh, fish and PowerShell and pairs `--json` with `--jq` and `--template`; Taskwarrior's reputation rests on the properties matiu names on HN — "Command line, so easy to integrate into other systems" and "It imports and exports JSON, so your data is never held captive". Against that, `cpath` has no completion at all, reads stdin only for `--password-stdin`, has exit codes (`EXIT.ok/usage/failure` in `cli/src/api/errors.ts`) that are real but undocumented and therefore unusable in a script, one flat config file with no profiles, and no access to the WebSocket stream the server already publishes on. The two with the highest ratio are completion (it is the signal that a CLI is finished) and stdin ingestion, which combines with the bulk-create row to give the whole product its migration path: pipe a meeting agenda or a spreadsheet column into a board in one command. `cpath watch` is the sleeper — the event catalog in README.md is already a stable contract, so exposing it as newline-delimited JSON turns realtime from a web-frontend detail into something people build on. Worth noting that Taskwarrior's `+BLOCKED` / `+BLOCKING` / `+READY` virtual tags are a direct competitor to your namesake feature, and `+BLOCKING` ("Does another task depend on this incomplete task?") is the thing Critical Path's `ready` command does not yet expose.

**Ownership transfer is a schema-level time bomb, and it needs fixing before account deletion is ever built.** `project.created_by` is NOT NULL with `ON DELETE CASCADE`, and creator access is implicit — never a `project_member` row. Those two facts combine badly. A naive account-deletion endpoint would cascade-delete every project the departing user created, including shared projects containing other people's work, with no warning and no recovery. And even without deletion, the flat model means the creator's role can never be handed over: the volunteer who set up the club's board leaves, and nobody can inherit it. Both halves are already documented in the wild by the closest comparable product — Nextcloud Deck #1306 asks for "New Owner for a board" (12 👍, motivated explicitly by team members leaving), and #1163 is the failure already happening: "Deleting a Board of a deleted user not possible." Wekan's own deletion spec (#2716) shows the shape of the answer: enumerate every board the user is in, and force a per-board choice between deleting the data and transferring it, with the last remaining admin protected. GitHub's repo transfer supplies the UX detail worth copying — the recipient confirms by email and the invitation expires, so ownership is never pushed onto someone silently. Sequencing matters here: make creator access an explicit, transferable thing first; ship transfer second; ship account deletion only third.

## Appendix — what the completeness critic said

Coverage of the *board* is close to exhaustive. I could not find a meaningful gap in cards, columns, dates, checklists, views, filters, search, notifications, automation, permissions, import/export, mobile/offline/keyboard, or dependencies — the dependency category in particular is the most complete piece of research here, and near-synonyms are already present for almost everything I probed (colour-coded stacks, blocked badges, ready views, leverage ranking, unblock notification, external blockers, WIP, aging, policies).

The blind spot is everything that is not a board feature. All ten researchers studied general-purpose GUI SaaS or GUI clones of it, so the corpus has: (a) nothing about the identity and account lifecycle that surrounds the board — how a collaborator who has no account gets in (Critical Path's `POST /:id/members/by-email` literally 404s on an unknown email, so today you cannot invite anyone who has not already signed up), how a user gets out (there is no account-deletion endpoint, and `project.created_by` is NOT NULL `ON DELETE CASCADE`, so deleting a user would destroy every shared project they created), no email verification, and no per-device session list; (b) nothing about multi-user *concurrency semantics* — 400 rows contain "realtime sync" five times and presence/edit-conflict zero times, even though Critical Path stores a rich-text description that two people can silently clobber; (c) nothing about the CLI as a product surface, despite it being the stated differentiator — no researcher studied a CLI-native tracker (Taskwarrior, todo.txt, `gh`, git-bug), so shell completion, stdin/batch ingestion, event streaming and exit-code semantics never appear; and (d) no vertical or domain-specialised tool was studied at all (HacknPlan and Codecks are game-development PM products with real users and real distinctive mechanics, and an HN commenter quoted in your own research explicitly advised specialising for game development).

Secondary bias worth naming: every researcher hit the same blocked Reddit and the same exhausted WebSearch budget (as did I), so the sentiment corpus is overwhelmingly HN + GitHub issues — i.e. self-hosting, speed, export, API. The explicit brief to cover non-software teams (nonprofits, clubs, freelancers, events) is the least-served part of the research, and the items below that touch it (invitations, accessibility, public input boards, asset index) are exactly where the evidence is thinnest in the existing corpus.
